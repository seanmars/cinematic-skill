# Noir

Summary: Motivated hard light, slanted venetian-blind shadows, wet glistening streets and faces half lost in shadow create a crime atmosphere with moral weight.

## Narrative Function

- Builds a crime atmosphere of guilt and judgment through deliberate shadow placement and high contrast.
- Unlike images that are merely "dark and moody", it has an explicit lighting grammar.

## How It Works

- Eye level or slightly low angle, 35mm, locked shot.
- Motivated hard key light, with a gobo casting slanted light patterns.
- Wet ground, the face half in shadow; one practical light source deep in the frame.

## When to Use

- Use: Crime stories, moments of moral struggle, urban scenes with a sense of judgment.
- Avoid: Sitcom interiors or soft-light scenes; misused, it only yields "a bit melancholy" rather than noir.

## Film Examples

- *The Third Man* (1949): Hard night light, wet cobblestones and moral shadows.
- *Drive* (2011): A color-era adaptation, optionally adding neon.

## Prompt Examples

```text
A detective waits in a rain-slick alley at night, 35mm slightly low, locked-off. A single hard key from a streetlamp throws venetian-blind slashes across the brick and cuts half his face into deep shadow, wet cobblestones reflecting one bare bulb far down the alley, crisp black-and-white contrast with true blacks.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A dark cyan photo with soft light and muddy blacks → Use motivated hard light and gobo slashes.
- Missing environmental cues → Add wet surfaces and let shadows deliberately obscure part of the face.
