# Cold Vision

Summary: A worldview driven by "temperature", either polar cold (visible breath, ice crystals, harsh winter light) or thermal-style false-color imagery.

## Narrative Function

- Conveys hunting, winter conflict or a stalker's point of view.
- Uses temperature logic to create emotional distance, making the image feel cold-blooded.
- Not suited to romantic or beauty scenes unless cruelty is intended.

## How It Works

- Choose one: polar photography (visible breath, ice crystals, cold hard winter light), or thermal false color (bodies "hotter" than the environment).
- Once chosen, stay consistent; never mix both approaches within one shot.
- Requires recognizable environmental physical cues, not just a cool grade.

## When to Use

- Use: Snow chases, surveillance stories, Cold War subjects.
- Avoid: Holiday romance, beauty lighting, or applying a cyan grade just for melancholy.

## Film Examples

- *The Revenant* (2015): Winter as a climate, with breath, ice and harsh daylight.
- *The Thing* (1982): Cold isolation interwoven with menace.

## Prompt Examples

```text
A hunter crouches in a frozen birch forest at dawn, breath steaming in thick clouds, ice crystals drifting through harsh low winter sun, cold steel-blue palette. His face stays readable under a frost-rimmed hood, the whole world rendered as if temperature were the only thing that mattered.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Only a cyan grade, with no environmental cues → Clearly choose the "climate" or the "device" route.
- Missing breath, ice or thermal edges → Include at least one visible piece of temperature evidence.
- Face unrecognizable → Keep features readable.
