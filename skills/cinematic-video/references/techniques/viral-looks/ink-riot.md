# Ink Riot

Summary: Liquid calligraphy and ink blooms explode and flow around a realistic subject, like weather in the air rather than a pasted-on texture.

## Narrative Function

- Externalizes a character's inner chaos and emotional turmoil as bursts of ink, while keeping the subject clearly readable.
- Suits title cards, music sequences or a character's breakdown, making them feel "like a page that fights back".

## How It Works

- Locked tripod or very slight handheld; eye level, 35-50mm, subject in sharp focus.
- Use practical sources (sodium lamps, window light, tungsten) as the key, so the body keeps photographic texture and shadow.
- Describe the ink as physical: bleeding, dripping, occupying the air; use a single ink palette, and the eyes must stay clear.

## When to Use

- Use: Title cards, musical interludes, scenes emphasizing a character's inner state.
- Avoid: Two-person dialogue that needs clean shot/reverse shot, procedural shots that need clear eyelines, scenes that must establish spatial geography.

## Film Examples

- *Hero* (2002): Zhang Yimou uses ink blooming in water as environmental weather.
- *Sin City* (2005): The opening titles use ink as a world-building element.

## Prompt Examples

```text
A dancer frozen mid-turn under a single sodium streetlight, 50mm lens, locked tripod, face sharp and photographic. Black ink erupts around her in airborne calligraphy strokes, blooming and dripping through the air like a storm, one ink palette only, her eyes kept clear of the ink.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Too much ink drowns the subject and the eyes disappear → Keep the face realistic and readable; ink surrounds but doesn't cover.
- Stacking halftone, oil paint and other effects at the same time → Use a single ink palette only.
- Erratic camera movement → Lock the camera so the ink is the only motion.
