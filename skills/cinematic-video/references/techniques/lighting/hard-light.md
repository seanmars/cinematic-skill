# Hard Light

Summary: A small, distant source produces sharp shadow edges, amplifying texture and contrast.

## Narrative Function

- Emphasizes texture, pores and sharp shadows, forming graphic high-contrast images.
- Linked to noir and westerns, conveying grit, interrogation or harsh circumstances.
- Unsuitable for tender or beauty-oriented expression.

## How It Works

- Use a source small relative to the subject: distant sun, bare bulb, focused Fresnel, snooted LED.
- The farther away, the smaller the apparent source and the harder the light.
- Usually white daylight or tungsten, with gels kept saturated; combine with flags for gobo patterns.

## When to Use

- Use: Noon street scenes, interrogations, noir situations, when surface texture must show.
- Avoid: Romantic close-ups, beauty shots needing gentle treatment, scenes expressing kindness or memory.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |

## Film Examples

- *The Big Combo* (1955) / *Se7en* (1995): Hard light carves faces and spaces.
- *Once Upon a Time in the West* (1968): Harsh sun outlines hats, dust and wood; shadow edges themselves become visual grammar.

## Prompt Examples

```text
High-noon western street: a small, distant sun under a clear sky casts razor-sharp shadows from hat brims and wooden posts, exaggerating every crease of weathered skin and grain of dusty timber. No diffusion, no wrap, stark noir-like contrast.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Mistaking high-contrast soft light for hard light (blurred shadow edges are the giveaway) → Shrink the apparent source, remove diffusion, increase distance, or use direct sun under a clear sky.
