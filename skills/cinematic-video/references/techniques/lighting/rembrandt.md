# Rembrandt Lighting

Summary: A high key at 45 degrees leaves a small triangle of light under the eye on the shadow-side cheek.

## Narrative Function

- Brings out a painterly, dimensional face without being as graphic as split lighting.
- Suits period interiors and serious close-ups, with the key imitating a high window or lamp.
- Unsuitable for beauty shots needing both cheeks lit, or when the character must seem fully candid and transparent.

## How It Works

- Key placed high, about 45 degrees off-axis, medium hardness or soft with some bite.
- The nose shadow extends down and joins the cheek shadow, enclosing a closed triangle of light under the shadow-side eye.
- Little or no fill; color temperature matches the source (warm interior or cool window light); often combined with short lighting.

## When to Use

- Use: Classical dramatic portraits, period close-ups, negotiation scenes.
- Avoid: When wrapping beauty light, evenly lit faces, or a half-face concealment effect is wanted.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Loop Lighting](loop-lighting.md) | Small nose shadow, not joined to the cheek shadow |

## Film Examples

- *Fanny and Alexander* (1982): Painterly window-light keys form the triangle pattern.
- *Road to Perdition* (2002): Conrad Hall captures Rembrandt-style faces in dark rooms.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Period drama close-up of a merchant by a tall window: the cool daylight key comes from high and about 45 degrees to the side, lighting his near cheek while the far cheek stays dark except for a small, isolated triangle of light under the shadow-side eye. The nose shadow joins the cheek shadow; almost no fill.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Nose shadow floats on the face without reaching the cheek, or the face is split down the middle with no triangle → Raise and rotate the key until the shadows connect, isolating a clear triangle under the far eye.
