# Short Lighting

Summary: The key lights the cheek turned away from the camera, while the near side facing the camera falls into shadow.

## Narrative Function

- Gives the face sculpture and mystery, with the mystery hidden on the camera-facing side.
- Suits noir and serious close-ups.
- Unsuitable for characters needing an open, public image.

## How It Works

- Turn the face 45-90 degrees away from the camera so the key lights the far cheek.
- The near cheek falls into shadow; adjust fill so the near eye stays visible (unless strong drama is wanted).
- Hardness depends on genre; visually slims and sculpts the face.

## When to Use

- Use: Close-ups in dramatic interiors (offices, bars), noir portraits.
- Use: Serious character moments needing dimension.
- Avoid: Comedic characters, or public occasions needing openness.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Broad Lighting](broad-lighting.md) | The opposite; lights the near cheek and feels open |
| [Rembrandt](rembrandt.md) | Classical pattern, often shot short, can also be broad |

## Film Examples

- *Road to Perdition* (2002): Sculpts characters' faces in dark rooms.
- *The Godfather* (1972)-lineage close-ups and classic noir portraits also often use the same approach.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Noir close-up in a dim bar: the man's face turns slightly away from camera, and a hard key lights only his far cheek. The camera-facing cheek falls into shadow while a faint fill keeps the near eye readable, giving a slim, sculpted, mysterious look.
```

## Common Mistakes

- The near cheek ends up bright (becoming broad or butterfly) → Turn the face, or move the key to the far side.
