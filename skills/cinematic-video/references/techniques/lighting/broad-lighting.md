# Broad Lighting

Summary: The key lights the near cheek facing the camera, making the face look open, bright and readable.

## Narrative Function

- Presents an open, public, easy-to-read image, suited to performances and stage occasions.
- Conveys a candid, approachable character.
- Unsuitable for mystery or menace.

## How It Works

- Key about 30-45 degrees off frontal, lighting the cheek nearest the camera.
- Fill can be generous.
- Usually softer than noir-style short lighting.

## When to Use

- Use: Open, readable portraits, public figures, comedic close-ups.
- Avoid: When the near cheek must stay in shadow or keep mystery.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Short Lighting](short-lighting.md) | Conversely lights the far cheek |
| [High-Key](high-key.md) | Controls overall contrast, not which cheek is lit |
| [Butterfly Lighting](butterfly-lighting.md) | Frontal on-axis, favoring neither side |
| [Loop Lighting](loop-lighting.md) | Can be broad or short depending on head angle |

## Film Examples

- *Singin' in the Rain* (1952): Classical portrait lighting for cheerful characters.
- *Roman Holiday* (1953): Likewise uses broad lighting to present open, likable characters.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Bright talk-show style portrait: the host's head turns slightly, and a soft key lights the camera-facing cheek and the wide side of the face. Generous fill keeps shadows shallow on the near side, for an open, friendly, easy-to-read look.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing it with short lighting so the near cheek goes dark → Move the key to the camera-facing side, or turn the face toward the source.
