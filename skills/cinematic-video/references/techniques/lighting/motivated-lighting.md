# Motivated Lighting

Summary: Every light appears to come from a plausible source in the story world, hiding the traces of lighting.

## Narrative Function

- Lets the audience feel a coherent story world rather than studio fixtures.
- Light appears to come from visible or believable sources: windows, lamps, signs, sky.
- Keeps lighting control while hiding technical traces; differs from pure natural light or visible practicals.

## How It Works

- Decide the story source first, then match the camera-side light direction to it.
- Hardness matches the source: distant sun = hard; sheer curtain = soft.
- Color temperature matches the source: tungsten about 2800-3200K, daylight about 5600K, sodium or neon kept at native color; supplemental lights follow the same axis and are flagged to the same throw direction.

## When to Use

- Use: Realistic scenes needing directional control, such as hotel corridors, kitchens, cars under streetlights.
- Avoid: Using it as an excuse for a frontal beauty key that violates spatial logic.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Practical Lighting](practical-lighting.md) | Fixtures visible in frame; motivated can be hidden supplemental lights |

## Film Examples

- *The Shining* (1980): Hotel fixtures and table lamps justify the interior light direction.
- *Children of Men* (2006): Windows, fixtures and skylight sustain a realistic world.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Night kitchen scene lit only as if by the story's sources: a cool 5600K moonlit window hard from camera-left and a warm 3000K range-hood bulb above the stove. Each beam's direction, hardness and color matches its fixture, with no unexplained frontal beauty light.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Passing off unmotivated studio three-point lighting as natural light, with light coming from walls that have no source → Name the story source explicitly and align the hidden fixtures with it.
