# Key Light

Summary: The dominant source that sets the subject's lighting direction, shadow shape and exposure baseline.

## Narrative Function

- Tells the audience where the light in this world comes from (window, bulb, sun).
- Conveys time of day, and how "exposed" or concealed the character is.
- It is the single dominant direction; if both sides are equally bright, there is no key.

## How It Works

- For portraits, usually 30-45 degrees off the lens axis and above eye line; split lighting places it at 90 degrees; butterfly is near-axis and high.
- Hardness depends on the apparent size of the source: small and distant → hard-edged shadows; large and diffused → wrap.
- Color temperature should match the story's source: daylight about 5600K, tungsten about 3200K, firelight about 1800K.

## When to Use

- Use: Any scene that must clearly establish "where the light comes from."
- Avoid: Adding an equally bright second key on the opposite side.

## Film Examples

- *The Godfather* (1972): A high, underexposed key leaves the eye sockets in shadow.
- *Apocalypse Now* (1979): Small, hard sources while the surrounding jungle stays dark.

## Prompt Examples

```text
A single hard key light from a bare tungsten bulb high on camera-right, about 3200K, carving the man's face into bright and shadowed halves. No competing light source; the far side of his face falls into deep shadow.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Fill so bright it becomes a second key (two nose highlights, unclear shadow side) → Lower or remove the fill until only one main direction remains.
- Prompt uses only vague mood words → State the source position, side, height, hardness and color temperature explicitly.
