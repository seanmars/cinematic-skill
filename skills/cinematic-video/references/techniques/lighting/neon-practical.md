# Neon Practicals

Summary: Neon tubes in frame actually serve as the key or strong accent, staining figures with their own magenta and cyan.

## Narrative Function

- Conveys a nighttime urban mood; signs are light sources, not just decoration.
- Wet ground reflects colored light back onto the subject.
- Mixed colored light feels real because real signs clash anyway; the nearest tube is the key, the next is the accent.

## How It Works

- Place actually lit tubes close enough to the subject to register exposure on camera.
- Softer along the tube's length, harder across it; forms specular highlights in eyes and on wet surfaces.
- Color depends on gas type and gels; wetting down the ground strengthens reflections.

## When to Use

- Use: Night cities, wet streets, motels, night markets, letting the city itself act as the lighting department.
- Avoid: A white key on the face against a neon-wallpaper background.
- Avoid: Daytime offices, or teal-orange grading with no tubes in frame.

## Film Examples

- *Blade Runner* (1982): Signs and tubes act as the key for interiors and streets.
- *Chungking Express* (1994): Hong Kong neon hits faces in mixed tones.
- *Drive* (2011): Magenta and cyan signs on wet night streets actually take part in lighting.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Rainy night alley: a magenta neon sign just left of frame keys the woman's cheek, while a cyan tube behind her adds a cold edge on her hair. Wet asphalt bounces both colors back up onto her coat; the signs are the real light sources, mixed and clashing like a real street.
```

## Common Mistakes

- White key with a neon bokeh background (neutral skin, a pile of colored bokeh behind) → Make the nearest tube the real key and allow mixed color on the face.
