# Fill Light

Summary: Lifts the shadows opposite the key, determining how much detail remains visible in the dark areas.

## Narrative Function

- Controls shadow readability: more fill feels open and public; less fill preserves mystery.
- Keeps the shadow-side eye and cheekbone readable without flattening facial dimension.
- Don't add it when a pure-black graphic look is needed, or room bounce is already enough.

## How It Works

- Placed opposite the key, with a larger, softer source to avoid a second set of shadows.
- Control intensity with distance, scrims or dimmers; common portrait key-to-fill ratios run from 2:1 (gentle) to 8:1 (lean).
- Neutral or slightly cool tone, so shadows don't turn orange under a warm key; wall bounce can also serve as fill.

## When to Use

- Use: Close-ups keyed by hard window light where the far eye and cheekbone must still be visible, e.g. interviews.
- Avoid: Scenes whose story needs clean, deep shadows.

## Film Examples

- *Casablanca* (1942): Portrait fill keeps both eyes clear.
- *The Age of Innocence* (1993): Soft interior fill keeps the room readable while the lamp sources still read.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Close-up lit by a hard window key from camera-left, with a large, soft, low-level fill about three stops under the key on the shadow side. Both eyes remain readable, but the far cheek stays noticeably darker, preserving a moody contrast ratio.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Fill becomes a second key on the other side (double nose shadows) → Enlarge the source, dim it, eliminate its shadows, or switch to bounce fill.
