# High-Key Lighting

Summary: Bright, low-contrast and shallow shadows, keeping the whole frame clean and open.

## Narrative Function

- Creates a sense of safety, cleanliness or artificial perfection, common in comedies, heavens, hospitals and candy shops.
- Allows saturated color; shadows never sink to black.
- Can be ironic: a bright scene paired with a sour performance exposes a false promise.

## How It Works

- Large sources plus generous fill, with light bouncing off walls and ceiling.
- Keep the contrast ratio at 2:1 or lower; the key still shapes, just with gentle falloff.
- Hold highlight detail on surfaces like skin and frosting; cool tones for sterile spaces, warm tones for candy worlds.

## When to Use

- Use: Scenes needing openness, such as comedic interiors, showrooms, hospitals and toy worlds.
- Avoid: Crime or murder scenes (unless deliberately ironic).
- Avoid: Equating it with overexposure, or using it in dark rooms.

## Film Examples

- *The Umbrellas of Cherbourg* (1964): Bright interiors sustain an artificial romance.
- *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968): The clean, bright spaces of the spaceship Discovery.
- *Marie Antoinette* (2006): Luminous, airy palace rooms.
- *Barbie* (2023): A precisely controlled bright Barbieland.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Bright high-key interior of a pastel bakery: large soft sources and generous bounce keep contrast low, around 2:1, with barely any shadows. White walls and frosting keep their texture, no blown highlights or glossy hotspots, cheerful and clean.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Blown whites, overexposed highlights, or a bright face dropped into a black space → Lower the contrast of the whole scene and preserve highlight texture.
