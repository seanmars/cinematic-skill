# Under-Light

Summary: A source below the chin shines upward, so shadows climb in reverse onto cheekbones and eye sockets, looking eerie.

## Narrative Function

- Inverts facial modeling: shadows climb upward, opposite to the normal direction of daylight.
- Linked to firelight, rituals or horror; reads as unnatural and unsettling.

## How It Works

- Place the source below the chin aiming up, close to the face to exploit fast inverse-square falloff.
- Quality is often hard light or a small soft source (e.g. a phone screen).
- Color is often warm firelight or cool screen light; a theatrical green also works.

## When to Use

- Use: When the source really is fire or a screen on the ground, or the threat comes from below the face.
- Avoid: Ordinary ceiling-lit dialogue or standard portraits; don't use it as beauty fill unless deliberately creating unease.

## Film Examples

- *Frankenstein* (1931): James Whale under-lights the monster for an inverted effect.
- *Hereditary* (2018): Low sources and ritual light create a similar eeriness.

## Prompt Examples

```text
A teenager kneels in a dark attic holding a flashlight just under her chin, pointing straight up. Hard light climbs her cheekbones and brow, throwing shadows upward into her eye sockets, while the source stays clearly below her face rather than off to the side.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Source placed to the side (e.g. a candle beside the face), producing a left-right split instead of upward-climbing shadows → Lower the source below the jawline and aim it up.
