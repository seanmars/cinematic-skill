# Three-Point Lighting

Summary: The key shapes, the fill opens the shadows, and the backlight traces the hair edge, making the face dimensional and separated from the background.

## Narrative Function

- The default grammar of classical portrait lighting; the audience sees both eyes and the facial structure clearly.
- Cheeks split into lit and shadow sides, hair separates from background, and the image gains depth.
- Keeps consistent readability across shot/reverse-shots and close-ups, suited to classical coverage.

## How It Works

- Key about 45 degrees off the lens axis, 30-45 degrees above eye line, usually soft.
- Fill several stops darker than the key, only opening shadow detail.
- Backlight high behind, often opposite the key, flag-controlled to trace only hair and shoulder line, avoiding flare.

## When to Use

- Use: Interview-style talking heads and studio close-ups that need consistent facial readability across shots.
- Avoid: Night interiors with only one real source.
- Avoid: Scenes where the story needs parts of the face to vanish into shadow.

## Film Examples

- *Casablanca* (1942): Classic Hollywood lighting keeps stars dimensional at every shot size.
- *Rebecca* (1940): The same studio portrait grammar, with faces consistent across shots.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Classic three-point lighting on a woman in a medium close-up: a soft key from 45 degrees camera-left and slightly above her eyeline, a dimmer fill gently opening the right cheek, and a warm backlight tracing her hair and shoulders against a dark backdrop. Sculpted, dimensional face with clear separation from the background.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Treating flat frontal light as three-point lighting: evenly lit cheeks, no hair edge, no nose shadow → Move the key off-axis, lower the fill, and add a flag-controlled rear rim light.
