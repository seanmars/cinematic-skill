# Golden Hour

Summary: Low-angle warm sun around sunrise or sunset, casting long shadows with amber directional light.

## Narrative Function

- Brief golden light conveys the passing of time.
- Lights ordinary scenes as "a moment that is slipping away."
- Unsuitable for scenes needing several continuous hours of coverage.

## How It Works

- Have the subject face or turn away from the low sun: backlight forms a warm rim and elongated ground shadows; it can also rake from the side or front.
- Light is softened by the atmosphere but keeps a clear direction.
- Color temperature is far warmer than at noon.

## When to Use

- Use: Early-morning or late-afternoon exteriors, shots that accept long shadows.
- Avoid: Faking it by grading noon sun orange.
- Avoid: Confusing it with post-sunset ambient light (blue hour).

## Film Examples

- *Days of Heaven* (1978): Prairie scenes make heavy use of light at the edge of day and night.
- *The Tree of Life* (2011): Naturally lit childhood sequences full of low-angle sunlight.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Two children run across a wheat field at sunset, the sun sitting just above the horizon behind them. Warm amber backlight rims their hair and the wheat tips, and long shadows stretch toward the camera. Natural low-sun warmth, not an orange filter on high daylight.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Treating blue hour or an orange-filtered noon shot as golden hour → Lower the sun, strengthen the warm key, and make the long shadows clearly visible.
