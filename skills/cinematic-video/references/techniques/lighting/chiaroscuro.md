# Chiaroscuro

Summary: A single source sculpts the figure's volume out of darkness; the strong contrast of light and dark carries moral weight.

## Narrative Function

- Uses strong light and dark to create moral, psychological or dramatic tension.
- Light reveals the character while darkness judges or hides, deliberately withholding information.
- Unsuitable for wide shots that need readable architecture, or comedies that need several faces visible at once.

## How It Works

- One motivated key (window, lamp, candle); reduce fill and flag off spill.
- Key slightly warm, slightly high, and small enough that brightness falls off quickly.
- Medium hardness; the planes of the face turn quickly, but eyes and hands stay readable.

## When to Use

- Use: Scenes where light carries moral meaning, such as confessions, crimes, doubt or late-night deals.
- Avoid: Daytime street scenes, shots that must fully show architecture, or when supporting characters' faces must be clear.

## Film Examples

- *The Third Man* (1949): In Vienna night scenes, figures are strategically hidden and revealed.
- *The Godfather* (1972): Contrast between the dark office and the bright wedding exterior.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Chiaroscuro portrait of a priest hearing a confession by a single candle: warm, compact light falls off fast, sculpting the planes of his face and hands while everything beyond arm's reach sinks into pure black. No fill, no ambient light, eyes still legible.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Merely underexposed and muddy overall, with no directional key → Establish a single warm source, flag off bounce, and expose the lit side correctly.
