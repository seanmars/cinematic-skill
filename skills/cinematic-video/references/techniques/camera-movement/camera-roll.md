# Camera Roll

Summary: The frame rotates continuously around the lens axis, rewriting what "up" means, as if gravity were negotiable.

## Narrative Function

- Turns a familiar space into a disorienting environment; bodies keep their weight while floors and walls keep swapping places.
- Most convincing when body mass and architecture remain plausible.

## How It Works

- The camera rotates continuously around the optical axis; or the camera stays fixed while the whole set rotates mechanically.
- Live action requires an engineered set, secured props and coordinated stunts.

## When to Use

- Use: Situations where gravity itself is the theme or optional, e.g. spaceships, dream logic, zero-gravity fights.
- Avoid: Low-key dialogue scenes that only need mild unease or a canted composition.
- Avoid: Post-production rotation that lacks spatial logic.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Dutch Roll](dutch-roll.md) | Rolls gradually to a cant and holds, not a continuous rotation |
| [360-Degree Orbit](orbit-360.md) | Circles the subject horizontally, horizon stays level |
| [Slow Zoom In](slow-zoom-in.md) | Intensifies focus without changing spatial orientation |

## Film Examples

- *Inception* (2010): The rotating-corridor fight, with the actors' bodies still carrying weight.
- *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968): Movement through a ring-shaped spaceship redefines up and down.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Continuous camera roll: the frame rotates steadily around the lens axis as two fighters tumble along a corridor whose walls become the floor. Their bodies keep believable weight while the architecture revolves, with gravity reorienting in one unbroken shot.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Treating a static Dutch Angle or 360-Degree Orbit as a camera roll → Must be a continuous rotation around the optical axis, or the whole room rotating until vertical orientation collapses.
- Inconsistent physics, with bodies like floating stickers → Keep body weight and architecture plausible.
