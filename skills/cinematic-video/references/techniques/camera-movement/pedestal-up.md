# Pedestal Up

Summary: The camera rises straight up a vertical column with heading and horizon unchanged; only eye height climbs.

## Narrative Function

- Changes height while keeping orientation and horizon stable.
- Presents a formal, architectural frame that feels visually steady.
- Unlike a crane, it doesn't bring a dramatic arc and pull back.

## How It Works

- The camera rises vertically along a fixed pedestal column or a virtual Y axis.
- Tilt and pan are locked throughout.
- Produces vertical parallax: the foreground sinks faster than the background.

## When to Use

- Use: Transitioning between heights while keeping eyeline direction, e.g. rising from tabletop to a standing speaker.
- Use: Architectural or studio scenes needing stable composition.
- Avoid: When a dramatic arc, a rise-and-pull-back reveal or a skyline discovery is needed.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Crane Up](crane-up.md) | Travels an arc, often with a pull back, changing several axes at once |
| [Tilt Up](tilt-up.md) | Height unchanged, only tilts up |

## Film Examples

- *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968): treats vertical position as spatial geometry.
- Multi-camera studio drama: the pedestal is the standard rise-and-lower rig.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Pedestal up in a straight vertical line: the camera starts level with a chessboard on a table and rises smoothly to the eye height of the standing player, tilt and heading locked the entire time. The table edge drops out of frame faster than the bookshelves behind, with no arc or backward drift.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Mistakenly made into a tilt up or crane up, with a tilting horizon or a backward arc → keep a straight Y-axis move and lock tilt.
