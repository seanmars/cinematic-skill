# Crane Up

Summary: The crane raises the camera from human height while retreating slightly, shrinking the character to a dot in a larger environment.

## Narrative Function

- Shifts from intimate human scale to a vast view of the environment.
- Reduces a person to a small figure in the world, emphasizing loneliness or vulnerability.
- The rise must reveal something meaningful; an empty climb has no power.

## How It Works

- A jib or Technocrane physically raises the camera while easing back.
- Keep foreground reference objects to maintain spatial continuity.
- Keep the subject in frame throughout so the audience doesn't lose orientation.

## When to Use

- Use: Scene endings, or placing a private moment in a larger context.
- Use: When the rise reveals new information such as a city, landscape or crowd.
- Avoid: When the top frame adds no information over the opening frame.

## Film Examples

- *Gone with the Wind* (1939): The camera rises over the Atlanta depot, revealing wounded soldiers everywhere.
- *The Player* (1992): Opening long take choreographed with a studio crane.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Crane up at golden hour with a 35mm lens: starting at waist height beside a lone soldier standing among ruins, the camera rises smoothly while drifting slightly backward, a broken wall staying in the foreground as an anchor. He remains visible as a tiny figure while the frame opens onto a vast devastated city.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Rendered as a tilt, or a hard cut to an aerial shot → Must be a continuous move of actual rising with a retreat.
- Subject disappears during the rise or no foreground reference → Keep a foreground anchor and the subject visible throughout.
