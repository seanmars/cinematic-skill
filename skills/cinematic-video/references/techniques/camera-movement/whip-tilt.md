# Whip Tilt

Summary: The lens whips up or down at extreme speed, smearing into vertical streaks midway, then slams to a stop on a new composition.

## Narrative Function

- Creates a vertical shock, unlike the horizontal impact of a whip pan.
- Deliberately hides the middle of the move under blur.
- Links two vertically separated frames with completely different compositions, e.g. a screaming mouth → a weapon below.

## How It Works

- The camera pitches rapidly around the horizontal axis, faster than the frame can be read.
- The tripod stays fixed, avoiding vertical camera displacement.
- End with a decisive stop on a composed frame; if cutting mid-move, match the blur.

## When to Use

- Use: Sudden vertical reveals or jump-scare moments.
- Use: When key action happens above or below the frame.
- Avoid: Measuring building height, gentle reveals, or cases needing actual crane travel.

## Film Examples

- *Evil Dead*: violent vertical whips as a language of shock.
- *Crank* (2006): similar speed conveys bodily panic.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Whip tilt: a close-up of a woman screaming, then the camera snaps straight down so fast the frame dissolves into vertical streaks of motion blur, stopping abruptly on a bloody knife lying on the floor at her feet. Tripod stays planted, no crane travel.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Streaks become horizontal, like a whip pan → make sure the blur is vertical streaking.
- Architecture or horizon still visible midway → rotate fast enough to blur completely.
- Loose landing or the tripod moving → keep the support fixed and stop on a sharp composition.
