# Pan Left

Summary: The camera stays fixed on a tripod and rotates left only around the vertical axis, carrying the eye from one place to another.

## Narrative Function

- Links two elements in the same space like a turn of the head.
- The viewer's position stays the same; only attention is guided.
- Not suitable when foreground sliding or parallax is needed.

## How It Works

- The tripod is locked in place, rotating horizontally only (yaw), with no body travel.
- The horizon stays level throughout.
- Compose both the start and end frames, with smooth rotation in between.

## When to Use

- Use: Two subjects in the same space, linked by "turning to look."
- Avoid: When a cut or tracking would be more effective.
- Avoid: When the camera needs to physically pass foreground objects.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |

## Film Examples

- *The Searchers* (1956): rotates from a fixed position, tying together riders, wilderness and the doorway.
- *The Rules of the Game* (1939): pans link characters in deep-focus compositions.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Smooth pan left from a locked tripod in a quiet train carriage: the shot opens on a man reading a newspaper, then rotates slowly leftward, horizon level, to settle on a woman across the aisle secretly watching him. Pure rotation, no camera travel, clean composed end frame.
```

## Common Mistakes

- The body moves too, becoming a dolly left → keep the tripod fixed and rotate only around the vertical axis.
- Tilted horizon or loose end composition → keep level and design start and end frames in advance.
