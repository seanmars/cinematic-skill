# Crane Over

Summary: The crane rises and moves forward over the subject while the lens tilts down, ending on a near-vertical downward view.

## Narrative Function

- Turns the scene into a spatial diagram without cutting.
- The audience experiences the continuous change in height and angle firsthand.
- Characters and objects become a readable floor plan.

## How It Works

- The crane rises while arcing forward.
- As it passes over the subject, the remote head gradually tilts down.
- Ends on a near-overhead angle.
- Watch for the crane's shadow and changes in window light from the new angle.

## When to Use

- Use: Turning blocking into a diagrammatic overhead view, e.g. a dinner-table argument becoming a chess game.
- Avoid: When the ending is still forward-facing, or when only a static top-down shot is needed.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Crane Up](crane-up.md) | Rises but stays forward-facing; doesn't turn into a high angle |
| [Overhead](../camera-angles/overhead.md) | Static top-down shot with no dynamic arc |
| [Slow Zoom In](slow-zoom-in.md) | Shifts attention without changing spatial position |

## Film Examples

- *The Untouchables* (1987): The Union Station staircase scene uses an overhead move to turn the action into a diagram of stairs and bodies.
- *Birdman* (2014): Arcs into a high angle, revealing both the theater layout and the characters' paths.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Crane over a long banquet table mid-argument: the camera rises from shoulder height and arcs forward over the diners while steadily tilting down, in one unbroken move, finishing almost straight overhead so the guests and dishes read like pieces on a board.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Ending on a forward-facing skyline, or cutting to an overhead shot → One continuous crane path, ending at a steep high angle with the subject recognizable.
