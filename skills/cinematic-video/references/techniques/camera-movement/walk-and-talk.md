# Walk and Talk

Summary: The camera accompanies characters talking while walking in a medium shot, the architecture flowing past with the dialogue.

## Narrative Function

- Moves information and space together, advancing dialogue and geography at once.
- Doorways and corners become punctuation in the dialogue rhythm.

## How It Works

- Usually Steadicam or gimbal, leading backward in front of the characters or moving alongside.
- Hold a moving medium shot at shoulder height, keeping headroom.
- Extras and sound equipment must be worked into the blocking path.

## When to Use

- Use: When large amounts of information must be delivered while moving through a workplace or corridor.
- Avoid: Contemplative seated dialogue.
- Avoid: Pure action or static scenes without dialogue; characters must walk and talk at the same time.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Tracking Shot](tracking.md) | Broader moving follow, can be dialogue-free action |
| [Steadicam](steadicam.md) | Long passages through architecture, not limited to dialogue |
| [Slow Zoom In](slow-zoom-in.md) | Adds tension without changing spatial position |

## Film Examples

- *The West Wing* (1999): the corridor walk and talk became the whole series' signature style.
- *The Social Network* (2010): dialogue while walking combines status relations with campus architecture.
- *Birdman* (2014): continuous dialogue sequences strung together in long takes.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Walk-and-talk: a gimbal camera backs up ahead of two doctors striding through a busy hospital corridor, holding a medium two-shot at shoulder height as they argue. Their faces stay readable while doorways, nurses and gurneys flow past.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Done as a dialogue-free follow, or a static two-shot → characters must talk while walking, with readable faces and visible spatial transitions.
- Camera trailing behind the characters, catching only their backs → lead from the front or move alongside.
