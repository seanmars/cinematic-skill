# Tracking Shot

Summary: The camera travels parallel to a moving subject at a constant distance, with foreground, midground and background sliding past.

## Narrative Function

- Accompanies a moving subject, keeping a consistent distance and viewpoint.
- Keeps both the subject and surrounding space, showing duration and physical exertion.
- Conveys fatigue, cause and effect, and spatial continuity in a way a static shot cannot.

## How It Works

- Move along a parallel path via dolly, Steadicam, gimbal or vehicle.
- Keep distance and shoulder height consistent unless the story requires otherwise.
- Lateral move in a single direction, with clear parallax across foreground, midground and background.

## When to Use

- Use: Walking, running or fighting sequences needing spatial continuity, corridor scenes, physically exhausting action.
- Avoid: Seated dialogue or interrogations, static subjects, scenes where movement is incidental.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Follow](follow.md) | Follows from behind, shooting the back of the head; tracking is a side-on view |
| [Steadicam](steadicam.md) | A rig for achieving tracking, not a standalone camera move |
| [Walk and Talk](walk-and-talk.md) | Applies tracking to dialogue sequences |

## Film Examples

- *Oldboy* (2003): the narrow-corridor hammer fight, mainly lateral side-on movement.
- *The 400 Blows* (1959): Antoine runs toward the sea.
- *Goodfellas* (1990): Henry leads Karen through the back of house into the nightclub.
- *The Shining* (1980): Danny rides his tricycle through the hotel corridors.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Side-on tracking shot at shoulder height: the camera glides alongside an exhausted marathon runner at exactly her pace, keeping a constant distance. Spectators and barriers in the foreground whip past, the street mid-ground drifts, and distant buildings crawl, all moving in one direction.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Faking it with a fixed-tripod pan or zoom, with no foreground sliding → use a moving support, match the subject's speed, and let near objects sweep past visibly.
