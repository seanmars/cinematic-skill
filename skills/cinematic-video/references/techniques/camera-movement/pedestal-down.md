# Pedestal Down

Summary: The camera descends straight down a vertical column with tilt unchanged, settling smoothly back to human eye level.

## Narrative Function

- Brings the shot back to human eye level after a formal high composition.
- Tilt stays fixed during the descent, so frame orientation is unchanged.
- Creates a steady, solemn descent rather than a dramatic dive.

## How It Works

- Moves only along the vertical (Y) axis.
- Tilt stays unchanged; yaw is usually fixed too.
- Produces reverse vertical parallax: architectural elements appear to climb in frame.

## When to Use

- Use: Descending from chandelier height to a character while keeping the same viewing angle.
- Use: Changing camera height without relying on tilt to keep the subject in frame.
- Avoid: When the descent also needs to look down, or the camera arm must clear obstacles.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Crane Down](crane-down.md) | Travels an arc and moves forward; tilt changes on arrival |
| [Pedestal Up](pedestal-up.md) | Opposite direction, smooth rise |
| [Tilt Down](tilt-down.md) | Height unchanged, only tilts down |

## Film Examples

- *Barry Lyndon* (1975): favors geometrically rigorous formal descents.
- *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968): treats vertical position as a physical fact of the scene.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Pedestal down: the camera descends in a perfectly straight vertical line from the height of a crystal chandelier to the eye level of two diplomats shaking hands below, tilt angle frozen and heading unchanged. The ballroom columns appear to rise in frame as the camera lowers, with no arc or nod.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Mistakenly made into a tilt down or crane down, with the horizon nodding or the path curving → descend in a straight vertical line, lock tilt, and stop at the new eye level.
