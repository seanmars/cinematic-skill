# Crane Down

Summary: The camera descends on a crane from a high omniscient view, closing in to intimate contact at human scale.

## Narrative Function

- Transitions directly from an overview to intimate contact with the character.
- Maintains narrative continuity without a cut.
- The descent itself is the narrative, narrowing the spatial scope step by step.

## How It Works

- The crane physically lowers the camera from height, often while pushing forward.
- A remote head holds the composition throughout.
- When height, direction and horizon align at the handoff point, it can connect seamlessly to a handheld or walking shot on the ground.

## When to Use

- Use: Moving from a wide view into a personal situation in one continuous move.
- Use: Finding a specific subject within a large environment.
- Avoid: When a static high-angle shot is enough or a straight cut is cleaner; don't scan around during the descent.

## Film Examples

- *Bound for Glory* (1976): Descends into a migrant workers' camp, then continues moving at ground level.
- *Contact* (1997): Descends over the VLA observatory, connecting to Ellie walking toward the building.
- *I Am Cuba* (1964): Long, complex descents in the hotel and funeral sequences.
- *Notorious* (1946): Descends across a party all the way to the key in the heroine's hand.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Crane down from high above a crowded night market, the camera descending continuously while gliding forward, horizon held steady. It settles at conversational distance on an old woman handing a lantern to a child, ending at eye level.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Looks like a tilt down, or height jumps abruptly → Ensure height changes continuously.
- Inconsistent horizon, unreadable landing point → Align the horizon and end on a clear human-scale composition.
