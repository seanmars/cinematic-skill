# Pan

Summary: The camera rotates horizontally from a fixed position, the frame sweeping left or right with the horizon kept level.

## Narrative Function

- Connects two separate elements from the same standpoint without moving the camera itself.
- Can reveal, follow, foreshadow or delay action.
- Unmotivated continuous panning weakens narrative force.

## How It Works

- Mounted on a tripod or locked remote head, rotating only around the vertical axis (yaw).
- Keep the horizon level unless deliberately doing a Dutch angle.
- Speed can be a slow crawl, follow pace, or a fast whip; fast pans create edge smearing rather than spatial distortion.

## When to Use

- Use: Linking two things from a single viewpoint, e.g. gun and face, window and street, speaker and listener.
- Avoid: Substituting for tracking that requires camera movement.
- Avoid: Casual reframing with no narrative purpose.

## Film Examples

- *12 Angry Men* (1957): pans reframe the jurors' positions, echoing shifting allegiances.
- *Rear Window* (1954): pans from a fixed window to survey the courtyard and apartments.
- *Eyes Wide Shut* (1999): reframes by following dance partners.
- *The Stendhal Syndrome* (1996): panoramic sweeps in the museum.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Slow pan from a fixed tripod position: the camera rotates horizontally from a detective standing by the window to the unopened letter lying on the desk across the room. Level horizon, no camera travel, only a smooth yaw rotation linking the two.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Done as trucking, with foreground shifting → lock the camera position, rotate only around the yaw axis, keep level, and connect subjects by angle rather than travel.
