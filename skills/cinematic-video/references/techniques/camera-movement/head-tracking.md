# Head Tracking

Summary: The lens is locked to the head; the face stays fixed while the background revolves around the character as they turn.

## Narrative Function

- The face is the only stable element while the surroundings keep changing, creating both disorientation and intimacy.
- Suggests the character is disconnected from a changing world.

## How It Works

- Uses a helmet rig, a face-mounted rig, or digital head locking.
- The face stays sharp and centered.
- When the subject turns, the room revolves around them, rather than the camera panning conventionally.

## When to Use

- Use: When the face must stay stable while the surroundings flow and change like weather.
- Avoid: Faking it with a long-lens pan from a distance; the face must be truly pinned in frame.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |

## Film Examples

- *Gravity* (2013): Head-locked framing with debris orbiting around.
- *Enter the Void* (2009): Head-locked subjectivity presents the view from the center of consciousness.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Head-tracking shot: the camera is locked to a soldier's helmet, his face pinned sharp and centered as he whips around in a burning village, the flames, houses and running figures swinging around him in a full orbit. No extra zoom, only the world sliding during each turn.
```

## Common Mistakes

- The face drifts in frame, or the background doesn't shift when turning (faked by panning a tight shot from afar) → Lock to the head, pin the face, and let the room revolve.
