# Slow Zoom Out

Summary: The camera position stays still while focal length slowly shortens, letting the already-present environment enter the frame bit by bit.

## Narrative Function

- Reveals surroundings that were always there but previously unframed.
- Carries an observer's feel: the lens doesn't move, it just "allows" more information in.
- Suited to showing isolation, being surrounded by an institution, or a gag hidden in the scene.

## How It Works

- Tripod locked; turn the zoom ring in reverse, from long to short focal length.
- Compose the tight opening frame first, and plan the widest ending composition in advance.
- Depth of field naturally deepens as focal length shortens.

## When to Use

- Use: Slowly widening from a close-up to reveal the whole table watching the protagonist.
- Use: Establishing the larger environment or lonely situation the subject is in.
- Avoid: As a substitute for "leaving the room"; use a dolly when a physical retreat is needed.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Pull Out](pull-out.md) | Camera physically retreats, with parallax |
| [Dolly Out](dolly-out.md) | Retreats through space, leaving the foreground behind; a zoom keeps the original spatial relationships |
| [Slow Zoom In](slow-zoom-in.md) | Opposite direction, tightens the frame in place |

## Film Examples

- *Barry Lyndon* (1975): slowly widens from a detail into painterly social group portraits.
- *The Social Network* (2010), *Her* (2013), *Lost in Translation* (2003): optical widening places characters in an inescapable larger environment.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Slow optical zoom out from a locked tripod, starting on a tight close-up of a child's face and widening over several seconds to reveal a vast, empty train station around her. No camera translation and no parallax; the architecture and negative space simply fill in as the focal length shortens.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Shot as a dolly out, with foreground objects sliding → keep the body fixed and drive only the focal length.
- Ending abruptly → hold on the widest composition so the audience can absorb the new information.
