# Transitions

Summary: Design the cut itself as a visible gesture, making "how we leave this scene" part of the narrative.

## Narrative Function

- Visible joins carry meaning: a wipe is like turning a page, a match declares a concept, a dissolve evokes memory.
- The transition becomes part of the story rather than invisible continuity.

## How It Works

- Design both sides of the cut together, with intent.
- Choose one clear transition type: rhyme, blend, fade, moving edge, pointing, blur or spatial shift.
- Design A and B together so the chosen method has material to work with.

## When to Use

- Use: between acts, major world shifts, when the way of leaving carries thematic meaning.
- Avoid: using designed transitions on every cut of shot/reverse-shot dialogue.
- Avoid: when the scene should feel unedited, like found footage.

## Film Examples

- *Star Wars* (1977): directional wipes become serial-style grammar.
- *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968): a hard-cut match achieves the greatest leap in film history.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Designed transition between two scenes: a clock-hand wipe sweeps clockwise from 12 o'clock, replacing a quiet village square at noon with the same square flooded with lanterns at midnight. One clearly named transition type, crisp edge, no vague blending or morphing.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Designed as a dissolve but a hard cut accidentally shows → choose one transition and follow only its rules.
- An unmotivated morph appears between unrelated shots → explicitly specify the transition type and both sides.
