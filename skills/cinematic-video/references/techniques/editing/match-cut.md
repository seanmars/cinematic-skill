# Match Cut

Summary: Two self-contained shots joined by a hard cut through correspondence in shape, position, motion or sound, letting the audience "see" their conceptual link at the cut.

## Narrative Function

- The cut itself is the meaning: two seemingly unrelated images gain a thematic link through correspondence.
- Both shots stand on their own; the audience supplies the link in the gap.
- Often used to bridge huge leaps in time or space.

## How It Works

- Design the two images as a pair before shooting.
- Align the dominant outline, frame position, direction of motion or sound envelope.
- Execute with a hard cut, not a dissolve or morph.

## When to Use

- Use: conceptual leaps (tool becomes tool, flame becomes sun), images from two different times and places expressing the same idea.
- Avoid: when simply leaving a room or changing angle.
- Avoid: when the next shot is just an adjacent space.

## Film Examples

- *2001: A Space Odyssey* (1968): a tossed bone cuts along the same trajectory to a spacecraft.
- *Lawrence of Arabia* (1962): a blown-out match cuts to a desert sunrise at the same scale.
- *Apocalypse Now* (1979): a ceiling fan cuts to helicopter rotors.
- *Parasite* (2019): visual correspondences link spaces of different classes.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Match cut: shot A shows a spinning coin on a dark table, centered in frame; hard cut to shot B, a full moon rising at the exact same position and size. Two distinct objects linked by the same circular silhouette, no morph, no dissolve.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Made into a morph or dissolve → use a hard cut and keep the two objects distinct.
- Only the subject is the same, with no shared geometry → lock a shared outline or motion vector.
