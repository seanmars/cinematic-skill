# Crash Cut

Summary: Hard cut straight into an event or its aftermath with no setup, opening on a sense of collision.

## Narrative Function

- Starts inside the event or its aftermath, without first establishing context.
- Acts like percussion in the rhythm, refusing a soft opening.
- Builds immediacy through collision rather than introduction.

## How It Works

- Two fully valid shots joined by a hard cut, maximizing contrast in scale, brightness or intensity of action.
- The second frame is often mid-action.
- If time later rewinds, clear markers (title cards, visual shifts) are needed so the structure doesn't read as a mistake.

## When to Use

- Use: when impact must land immediately, or entering through the aftermath serves the story better.
- Avoid: between two similar wide shots.
- Avoid: when calm must be established before it is broken (use a Smash Cut), or only a few frames are inserted (use a Flash Cut).

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Smash Cut](smash-cut.md) | Breaks a previously established calm; a crash cut has no preceding calm |
| [Jump Cut](jump-cut.md) | Maintains similarity; a crash cut maximizes difference |
| [Flash Cut](flash-cut.md) | Too brief to become a new scene; a crash cut's frame stays |

## Film Examples

- *Goodfellas* (1990): opens on the car-trunk scene, explaining the backstory only later.
- *Fight Club* (1999): opens on a crisis scene, then traces back cause and effect.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Crash cut: hard cut from a black title card straight into the middle of a violent car crash, glass exploding toward the lens mid-impact. No fade, no dissolve, no setup shot; maximum contrast in scale and motion between the two frames.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A quiet setup placed first turns it into a smash cut → collide directly, with no preceding silence.
- The second frame flashes by and becomes a flash cut → both frames need weight and must stay.
