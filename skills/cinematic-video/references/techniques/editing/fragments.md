# Fragments

Summary: Break an event into many partial shots without ever showing the whole, letting the audience assemble the action in their heads.

## Narrative Function

- Constructs an event from partial views, with the audience completing the action.
- Heightens impact without showing it directly.
- Inference comes from the gaps between shots, not from continuous action.

## How It Works

- Break the event into elements that can be shot safely: an approaching object, body parts, reactions, aftermath.
- Maintain consistent screen direction and a plausible order.
- Link the fragments with rhythm and sound so the audience fills the gaps unconsciously.

## When to Use

- Use: shower scenes, massacres shown through detail, panic sequences, fractured memories.
- Avoid: demonstrations that need clear continuous shots.
- Avoid: legal or documentary uses that require a complete record of the action.

## Film Examples

- *Psycho* (1960): the shower murder is built from fragmentary shots and sound, not continuous violence.
- *Battleship Potemkin* (1925): the Odessa Steps is built from details and reaction shots.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Fragmented editing of a break-in: a gloved hand on a doorknob, a splintering lock, a woman's eyes snapping open, a shadow across a hallway rug, a fallen lamp. Only partial close shots in causal order, bound by one continuous sound bed, never a wide shot showing the whole event.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Fast-cutting complete wide shots → deliberately withhold full contact and show only parts.
- Made into a collage texture, or a master shot already explains everything → keep causal logic and let sound and inference complete the action.
