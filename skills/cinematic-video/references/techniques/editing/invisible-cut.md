# Invisible Cut

Summary: Hide the cut in motion, darkness, occlusion, blur or a digital seam, making multiple shots look like a single continuous take.

## Narrative Function

- Creates the illusion of a single continuous shot, sustaining audience immersion.
- Useful for vanishing tricks, time jumps within a long take, or long walks through multiple spaces.

## How It Works

- Find an occluder: a passing body, a dark surface, whip pan blur, a matching doorframe.
- Lock exposure and lighting consistently across both shots.
- Live action: keep the camera fixed, swap objects during the occlusion and cut at the occlusion point; Digital: match motion blur and grain at the seam.

## When to Use

- Use: when the cut must go completely unnoticed.
- Avoid: using it to dodge scene construction, or when the cut itself should convey meaning.
- Avoid: when geography needs to change visibly on screen; use a Set Transition instead.

## Film Examples

- *The Vanishing Lady* (1896): a substitution trick makes a woman vanish between frames.
- *Rope* (1948): dark foreground objects hide the cuts between long takes.
- *1917* (2019): trenches and ruins appear continuous but conceal cuts.
- *Birdman* (2014): theater and city sequences flow continuously, hiding time jumps.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Seamless invisible cut: the camera tracks behind a soldier through a trench, a dark wooden beam passes close in the foreground and fully fills the frame, and on the other side the same soldier emerges into a ruined village at dusk. Lighting, grain and camera motion stay continuous, no liquid morphing.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Grain shifts, jumping crowds or warping walls expose the seam → use an occluder that truly fills the frame.
- Inconsistent lighting → match lighting precisely and bracket the cut with continuous motion on both sides.
