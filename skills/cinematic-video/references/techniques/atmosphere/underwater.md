# Underwater

Summary: The camera is submerged; the surface becomes a ceiling, light falls in rippling caustic nets, and bodies and clothing move slowly in buoyancy.

## Narrative Function

- Caustics and buoyancy define the entire frame.
- Light falls in a moving grid from the surface, which becomes a "ceiling."
- No fixed horizon; bodies move against water resistance, and clothing and hair lag behind.

## How It Works

- Put the lens in a waterproof housing and submerge it (or shoot in a tank).
- Expose using caustics from real sunlight passing through the surface.
- Backlight bubbles and suspended silt.
- Costumes and hair must float naturally.

## When to Use

- Use: Diving, drowning, flooded spaces, aquatic life.
- Avoid: Faking underwater with a blue grade in a dry interior.
- Avoid: When the surface and sky are story elements (use ocean instead), or as a substitute for slow motion in air.

## Film Examples

- *The Big Blue* (1988): Truly lives in the medium of water rather than just tinting blue.
- *The Shape of Water* (2017): Tanks and canals become a second gravity.
- *Thunderball* (1965): Stages the mise-en-scène around real underwater geography.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Submerged camera three meters below the surface: rippling nets of sunlight caustics dance across a young diver's face and body as she drifts weightlessly, her hair and white dress floating slowly around her. Backlit bubbles and specks of silt hang in the blue-green water, and the bright surface above acts as a shimmering ceiling with no horizon.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Dry actors in air with a blue grade, clothing hanging normally and a skyline still visible → Actually submerge the camera and let clothing float.
- Missing the optical traits of water → Add caustics and backlit suspended silt, and remove the in-air horizon.
