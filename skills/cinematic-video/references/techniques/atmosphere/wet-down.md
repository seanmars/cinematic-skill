# Wet-Down

Summary: The pavement is wetted into a mirror so night lights double in reflections on the ground, but no rain falls through the air.

## Narrative Function

- Turns the pavement into a reflective surface, doubling night light sources.
- A ground treatment for on-screen sheen, not a weather simulation.

## How It Works

- Spray water along the blocking path until a film of water forms.
- Arrange practical light sources (sodium lamps, neon, storefront windows) so their reflections enter the frame.
- Place the camera low to capture the ground's mirror effect.

## When to Use

- Use: Night driving, sidewalk sequences, neon districts.
- Avoid: Daytime use (unless the story justifies it).
- Avoid: As a substitute for falling rain, or when dry pavement with a glossy grade is enough.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Rain](rain.md) | Visible drops in the air; with wet-down the air stays clear |
| [Neon Practicals](../lighting/neon-practical.md) | The light source itself; wet-down is the medium that reflects it |
| [Fog](fog.md) | Diffuses light in the air; wet-down reflects it off the ground |

## Film Examples

- *Drive* (2011): Wet asphalt and practical lights form the Los Angeles night.
- *Collateral* (2004): Wet night streets reflect points of city light.
- *Who Framed Roger Rabbit* (1988): The studio wets down the ground to double streetlight reflections.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Low-angle night shot of an empty downtown street after the rain has stopped: the asphalt is soaked into a dark mirror reflecting red and blue neon signs and a row of sodium streetlamps. The air is completely clear with no falling drops, as a lone car rolls through the glistening reflections.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confused with rain, with rain streaks appearing → Keep the air clear unless rain is intended.
- Dry pavement with only a glossy grade → Actually wet it down to form a water film, and arrange light sources to be reflected.
