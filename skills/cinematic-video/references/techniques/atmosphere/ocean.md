# Ocean

Summary: The sea is a character, not a backdrop; rhythmic swells and salt spray set tiny people and boats against the entire horizon.

## Narrative Function

- The sea is an actor, not a backdrop.
- Creates tension through scale contrast (people / boats against the horizon).
- Conveys exile, labor, awe and conflict at sea; swells have rhythm, and spray signals weather and danger.

## How It Works

- Establish scale with wide shots.
- Show active swells (whitecaps, long swells with a clear period).
- Backlight the spray and salt mist; allow atmospheric haze in the distance.
- Keep the subject small relative to the water; allow real splashes on the lens and avoid an artificial "silky" surface.

## When to Use

- Use: Sea crossings, lighthouses, conflict at sea, surface sequences before and after a dive.
- Avoid: Mirror-calm water backdrops, fashion shoots posed on still lakes, harbors or pools without wind or waves.
- Once the lens goes below the surface, switch to underwater.

## Film Examples

- *The Big Blue* (1988): The sea surface is both workplace and temptation.
- *The Lighthouse* (2019): Two figures against indifferent water and rock.
- *Dunkirk* (2017): Moles, hulls and swells become hostile architecture, emphasizing weight and weather.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Wide shot of a tiny fishing boat climbing a massive rolling swell on a gray open sea, the waves arriving in a steady visible rhythm with whitecaps breaking along their crests. Low backlight turns the blowing salt spray into a glowing mist, a hard horizon line cuts across the frame, and droplets spatter the lens.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A calm stock seascape with posed models filling the frame and mirror-smooth water → Shrink the human scale and add periodic waves.
- No spray → Backlight the salt-mist particles and keep a crisp horizon.
