# Rain

Summary: Backlight makes rain streaks flash as silver lines in the air while wet ground reflects, adding a textured layer of weather to the frame.

## Narrative Function

- Makes the "air" visible through backlit water streaks.
- The ground becomes a reflective surface, doubling the light.
- Adds a textured weather layer to the scene; it only works with both falling drops and wet ground.

## How It Works

- Rig a water source in front of the camera (rain bars, rain towers or real rainfall).
- Light the drops with backlight or side light so the light scatters toward the lens.
- Keep the background darker so the streaks separate.
- Wet the ground even for light rain; shutter angle determines whether rain reads as long streaks or droplets.

## When to Use

- Use: Night streets, rooftops, prison yards and other scenes that need layers of falling water.
- Avoid: When you only need glossy wet surfaces (use wet-down instead), or a still veil of fog (use fog instead).
- Avoid: Bright overcast days without backlight, where rain becomes completely invisible.

## Film Examples

- *Blade Runner* (1982): Backlit rain streaks and reflective streets set the tone for the night scenes.
- *Se7en* (1995): Rain is a constant layer throughout the investigation.
- *The Shawshank Redemption* (1994): Arms outstretched in a downpour, a moment of emotional release.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Night city street in heavy rain: a strong backlight from a streetlamp behind the scene turns falling drops into bright silver streaks against a dark background. The asphalt is soaked and mirrors neon signs in rippling puddles as a man with an umbrella crosses through layers of rain in depth.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Streets glossy and wet but no rain streaks in the air, becoming a wet-down → Add airborne drops and backlight.
- The ground is still dry → Thoroughly wet the ground; no dry asphalt should appear.
