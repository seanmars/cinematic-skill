# Smoke

Summary: Smoke from cigarettes, incense burners or battle gives interior air volume that light can sculpt into beams and swirls.

## Narrative Function

- Makes the air in a room visible through combustion particles with a clear source.
- Creates a volume that light can sculpt into beams and eddies.
- Distinguishes spaces through tangible atmosphere: apartments, temples, war zones.

## How It Works

- Choose a source (cigarette, incense, battlefield, chimney).
- Control airflow to form directional ribbons of smoke.
- Side light or backlight reveals volume while keeping a clear line of sight to faces.
- Use hard beams to create light-shaft effects.

## When to Use

- Use: Interiors, temples, engine rooms, aftermath scenes.
- Avoid: Using it as generic atmosphere in a sunny kitchen.
- Avoid: Weather fog with no fire source, or cases that are actually steam or mist.

## Film Examples

- *In the Mood for Love* (2000): Ribbons of cigarette smoke linger in cramped interiors.
- *Blade Runner* (1982): Interior beams cut through smoke-filled air.
- *Apocalypse Now* (1979): Battlefield and jungle air becomes sculpted volume.

## Prompt Examples

```text
A dim jazz bar at night: a woman exhales from a cigarette and the smoke curls upward in slow ribbons, catching a hard backlight beam from a single spotlight. The room air holds a soft volume of haze, but her eyes and face stay clean and readable.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Unlit smoke muddies skin tones → Light the smoke with side light or backlight and keep faces clean.
- Looks like fog with no identifiable source → Add a recognizable smoke source.
