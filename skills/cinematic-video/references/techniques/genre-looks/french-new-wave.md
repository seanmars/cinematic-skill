# French New Wave

Summary: Shoots fiction with documentary-style real locations, available light and handheld camera, then shows the auteur's restlessness through jump cuts and looks into the lens.

## Narrative Function

- Conveys youthful restlessness and impatience with polished studio conventions.
- Characters may speak or look directly into the camera.
- City streets are a narrative element in themselves, not a backdrop.

## How It Works

- Real-location interiors with available light and a few practicals.
- 35mm or 50mm lenses, handheld or on a lightweight tripod.
- Jump cuts replace traditional coverage; add self-referential moments such as looks to camera, abrupt inserts, and street daylight mixed with cafe tungsten.

## When to Use

- Use: Restless fiction that needs street locations and editing urgency.
- Avoid: Polished studio productions, camcorder horror premises.
- Avoid: Scenes better served by traditional coverage.

## Film Examples

- *Breathless* (1960): Street jump cuts, in-car sequences and looks into the lens.
- *The 400 Blows* (1959): A childhood story shot on location, with available light and a free-spirited final sequence.
- *Cléo from 5 to 7* (1962): Moves through Paris streets and interiors in near real time.

## Prompt Examples

```text
French New Wave style, 1960s black and white 35mm: a young man in a rumpled suit walks a busy Paris boulevard with a woman selling newspapers, shot handheld on a 35mm lens in natural daylight, then he turns and glances straight into the lens. Cafe tungsten mixed with street light, no rim light, casual kinetic framing.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing it with Cinéma Vérité or found-footage horror (adding timestamps) → Keep fictional authorship.
- Using glossy three-point lighting → Keep location authenticity, handheld or a lightweight tripod, and jump cuts instead of continuous coverage.
