# Neo-Noir

Summary: Keeps film noir's composition and hard light but swaps silver tones for neon, sodium-vapor and rainy-night colored practicals.

## Narrative Function

- Uses color instead of classic noir's silver tones to express urban entrapment and moral ambiguity.
- Rain and neon take over the job of black-and-white contrast, keeping compositions of isolation and guilt.

## How It Works

- Hard key light 45-90 degrees off-axis, with colored practicals such as neon, sodium-vapor and tungsten serving as fill.
- Maintain a high contrast ratio.
- Often 35-40mm anamorphic or 35mm spherical lenses; locked wide shots or slow dolly-ins, occasionally pushing in on a face.

## When to Use

- Use: Contemporary or period crime scenes that need melancholy and rain, without 1940s monochrome aesthetics.
- Avoid: True period film noir, futuristic tech noir, pastel nostalgia styles.

## Film Examples

- *Chinatown* (1974): Period color, harsh daylight, venetian-blind interiors.
- *Blade Runner* (1982): Rain, smoke and neon key light.
- *Drive* (2011): Night exteriors, pink and teal practicals, frozen melancholy.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Neo-noir night scene: a lone driver sits in a parked car on a rain-soaked street, a hard magenta neon sign keying one side of his face while the other side falls into unfilled shadow. Sodium-orange streetlights reflect in wet asphalt; locked wide shot, 40mm anamorphic, high contrast.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Making it monochrome → That is Film Noir; add named neon or sodium-vapor sources.
- Only applying a flat teal-orange grade with no motivated sources → Keep a hard-light contrast ratio and wet down the ground.
