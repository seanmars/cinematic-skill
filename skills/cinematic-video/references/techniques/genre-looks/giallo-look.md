# Giallo

Summary: Saturated red, cobalt blue and green light, with leather gloves and blades in the foreground, turn lavish interiors into dangerous spaces.

## Narrative Function

- Horror comes from color design, not darkness.
- Threat enters elegant spaces through colored light, camera movement and foreground objects (gloves, blades).
- The opposite of film noir's restraint: color itself is weaponized.

## How It Works

- Primary-color gels (red, cobalt blue, green) shine the key light through windows or glass.
- High contrast plus high saturation.
- 35mm wide angle for interiors; slight Dutch angle and zooms at moments of threat; beauty light on the victim's face, surrounded by hostile color.

## When to Use

- Use: When stylish interiors need to turn threatening through color shifts and zooms.
- Avoid: Generic high-saturation horror, film-noir-style crime stories.
- Avoid: Purely decorative maximalism.

## Film Examples

- *Suspiria* (1977): Stained glass as motivated key light; the dance academy's architecture becomes a cage.
- *Deep Red* (1975): Zoom inserts and primary-colored interiors turn an investigation into fashionable horror.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Giallo-style interior: a woman in an elegant apartment is lit softly on her face while deep red light floods in through a stained-glass window behind her; a black leather glove holding a razor enters the foreground. Slow zoom toward her, slight Dutch tilt, saturated primaries and high contrast.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Making it a film-noir fedora night scene, or a loud grade with no gloves → Choose one primary color as the key light.
- Lacking threatening camera language → Zoom toward the threat or add a Dutch angle, and place a dark fashion object in the foreground.
