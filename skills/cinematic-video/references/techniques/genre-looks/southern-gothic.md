# Southern Gothic

Summary: Low afternoon sun rakes through moss and tree shadows, humid haze lifts the blacks, and peeling walls show decay and beauty at once.

## Narrative Function

- Conveys rot and beauty at the same time.
- The threat comes from "up close": family ties, historical trauma, the environment itself, not outside forces.
- Draws the audience psychologically close to place and relationships.

## How It Works

- Raking afternoon sunlight passes through moss and trees, forming moving shadow patterns.
- Haze or humidity turns blacks milky; use warm practicals such as porch lamps.
- 35mm / 50mm spherical lenses, occasionally 24mm to establish the landscape; keep surface weathering clearly visible in the highlights.

## When to Use

- Use: When heat, family dysfunction and environmental rot need to become tangible forces.
- Avoid: Urban night-crime narratives, cosmic-scale stories.
- Avoid: Stripping out Southern humidity, or applying it to European gothic architecture.

## Film Examples

- *The Night of the Hunter* (1955): River, moonlight and strong shadows.
- *Beasts of the Southern Wild* (2012): Daytime humidity, waterlogged environments and community fragility.
- *True Detective* (2014, Season 1): Sickly golden tones, an industrialized coast and family corruption.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Southern Gothic afternoon: a barefoot girl sits on the sagging porch of a peeling plantation house, low golden sunlight raking through Spanish moss and casting drifting shadows across her. Humid haze lifts the blacks, a warm porch lamp glows inside, 50mm lens, still and unhurried.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Applying the cold or silver tones of film noir or castle gothic, with no humidity → Switch to sickly afternoon light.
- Surfaces too clean, pacing too fast → Make humidity visible in the air and walls peeling, and slow the pacing to emphasize place and relationships.
