# BTS

Summary: Brings the crew, light stands, flags and the set's missing fourth wall into frame, admitting that the film is manufactured.

## Narrative Function

- Reveals the constructed nature of film by including crew, gear and set structure in frame.
- Depending on context, it can be comic, poignant or uncomfortable.
- Acknowledges the "machine" instead of sustaining the illusion of a finished world.

## How It Works

- Juxtapose two lighting environments: motivated scene light + bare worklights.
- C-stands, flags, slates, the missing fourth wall and boom operators are visible in frame.
- Use observational handheld, or a wider locked-off frame, so crew and gear read clearly.

## When to Use

- Use: When "the making of" must itself become part of the story.
- Avoid: Street vérité with no film set.
- Avoid: Claiming to be behind the scenes while the fourth wall is intact and all gear is hidden.

## Film Examples

- *Day for Night* (1973): Filmmaking itself becomes the subject.
- *Living in Oblivion* (1995): The chaos of indie production played as comedy.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Behind-the-scenes shot on a film set: a wide handheld view of a candlelit Victorian parlor set with its fourth wall missing, a boom operator overhead, black flags on C-stands and a crew member holding a slate in the foreground. Warm scene lighting mixed with bare white worklights, unfinished set edges visible.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Shooting it like street vérité or polished production stills → Put the missing wall, flags and boom operator in frame.
- Gear is hidden → Keep the mixed color temperature lighting; do not hide the production machinery.
