# Documentary Look

Summary: Mixes observational footage, interviews and archive material, using ungroomed available light to create a sense that "this is evidence."

## Narrative Function

- Creates the illusion of "evidence" through mixed aesthetic choices.
- Builds tension between what subjects say and what the camera sees, usable for comedy or unease.

## How It Works

- Leave mixed color temperatures uncorrected; window light serves as key for interviews.
- 35-50mm lenses at eye level; zoom focus is fine if a real crew would search for focus.
- No hair or rim light unless the room already provides it; the interviewer is usually offscreen.

## When to Use

- Use: When authenticity matters more than polish, or a fiction needs irony between interview and observation.
- Avoid: Using it as random camera shake.
- Avoid: Found-footage horror aesthetics.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |

## Film Examples

- *This Is Spinal Tap* (1984): Interviews and backstage observation build a fictional band.
- *Best in Show* (2000): Interviews and observational scenes develop an ensemble cast.
- *The Act of Killing* (2012): Reenactments plus interviews form an evidentiary structure.
- *Honeyland* (2019): Patient observation and mixed natural light.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Documentary-style interview: a middle-aged beekeeper sits in her cluttered farmhouse kitchen, lit only by a side window, answering an offscreen interviewer, mixed daylight and warm tungsten left uncorrected. 40mm lens at eye level, no rim or hair light, followed by handheld observational B-roll of her hives.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Adding timestamps to "documentary" footage → That is found footage; remove it.
- Using Hollywood-style rim and hair light → Keep a consistent crew perspective, preserve color temperature differences, and let windows and ambient sources do the lighting.
