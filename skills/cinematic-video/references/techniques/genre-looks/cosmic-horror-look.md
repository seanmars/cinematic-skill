# Cosmic Horror

Summary: Dislocated perspective, incomprehensible scale and unnatural light make humans look small and superfluous in frame.

## Narrative Function

- Makes the frame itself feel "wrong" to build unease: horizons don't line up, people look like mistakes.
- Underlighting turns familiar faces into specimens.
- The threat comes from scale and unknowable geometry, not a visible villain.

## How It Works

- Open with an extreme wide shot of an alien landscape or malformed structure.
- Add subtle perspective cheats: forced angles, misaligned vanishing points.
- Light from below, or use overexposed, sourceless illumination; palette leans toward Antarctic cold white or otherworldly iridescence.

## When to Use

- Use: When the threat exceeds human form and "seeing" itself is the danger.
- Avoid: Masked killers in hallways, family drama.
- Avoid: Ordinary gothic scenes with a visible villain.

## Film Examples

- *The Thing* (1982): Antarctic isolation, bodies that defy form.
- *Annihilation* (2018): Iridescent shimmer, mutation in daylight, the landscape itself is the threat.
- *Event Horizon* (1997): Fractured geometry and discomforting light inside the spaceship.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Cosmic horror extreme wide shot: a tiny lone researcher stands on a pale Antarctic ice plain before a colossal black monolith whose edges bend at impossible angles, the horizon subtly tilted out of true. Sourceless overexposed light with a faint iridescent shimmer, the human nearly lost in frame.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Falling into slasher tropes, visible human killers or generic fog → Widen the composition and shrink the human presence.
- Space is too normal → Add one geometric fracture and place the light somewhere unnatural (below or overexposed).
