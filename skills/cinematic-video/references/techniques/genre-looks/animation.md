# Animation

Summary: Light and motion are designed rather than captured by a lens, unlocking vocabulary live action cannot reach, such as smears, stepped frame rates, halftones and charcoal.

## Narrative Function

- Gains freedoms live-action photography lacks: smears, non-standard shutter, halftone graphics, dissolving charcoal lines.
- The camera is a viewpoint within a constructed world; lens physics applies only when deliberately chosen.

## How It Works

- Choose a construction method: 2D volume, CG with graphic shaders, or a hybrid.
- Lighting is deliberate compositional design, not motivated by practicals in the scene.
- Control motion with smears, holds, stepped frame rates (on 2s) or full 24fps; do not default to photoreal motion blur unless the story calls for it.

## When to Use

- Use: When world-building matters more than live-action capture.
- Avoid: When real lens physics is needed.
- Avoid: When the actual goal is a visible pixel grid (Pixel Art) or puppet animation (Stop Motion).

## Film Examples

- *Spider-Man: Into the Spider-Verse* (2018): Stepped motion, halftone print texture and designed lighting.
- *The Tale of the Princess Kaguya* (2013): Charcoal and watercolor become photographic elements.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Hand-drawn 2D animation: a girl leaps across rooftops at dusk, animated on twos with bold smear frames on her fastest motion, flat cel shading with a painted orange rim light as a graphic shape. Visible halftone dots in the shadows, no photoreal bokeh or lens blur.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Mixing in photoreal live-action, pixel-art or stop-motion traits (bokeh, puppet seams, chunky sprites) → Specify the graphic medium explicitly.
- Lighting and motion look like live action → Design the key light as paint or shader, and deliberately choose smears or stepped motion.
