# Found Footage Look

Summary: The images are presented as tapes shot by a character in the story and discovered afterward; the camera itself is inside the events.

## Narrative Function

- The camera becomes a story element with real limits: battery, timestamps, focus hunting.
- The audience knows only what the operator can capture; the operator is in danger, so framing is always a beat late and incomplete.
- Without an in-story source for the recording, it collapses into mere handheld or a cheap style.

## How It Works

- Choose a specific device (Hi8, MiniDV, early digital, phone) and match its traits: wide zoom range, auto-exposure jumps, rolling shutter, autofocus drift.
- Keep a consistent operator position and give a plausible reason for continuous recording.
- Avoid shots the character could not have taken (crane moves, perfect close-ups).

## When to Use

- Use: When the operator is inside the events and the tape could plausibly be discovered later.
- Avoid: Using shake only as a stylistic premise.
- Avoid: Omniscient amateur footage with no in-story operator.

## Film Examples

- *The Blair Witch Project* (1999): The narrative is built from recovered tapes of missing filmmakers.
- *REC* (2007): A TV station camera becomes the frame that constrains events.
- *Cloverfield* (2008): A personal camera records the disaster from a witness's point of view.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Found footage from a 2004 MiniDV camcorder held by a panicked college student filming his friends in a dark forest; timestamp burned into the corner, autofocus hunting, auto-exposure pumping as a flashlight swings past. Framing at chest height misses half the action, single camera only, no cutaways.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Handheld shake with no in-story operator, or perfect close-ups and crane shots the character could not capture → Choose a single device and lock the operator's position.
- Multiple camera angles appear → Keep only one camera; allow focus drift and framing that misses the action.
