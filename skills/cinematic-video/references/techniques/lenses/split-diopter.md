# Split Diopter

Summary: In one frame, a near subject and a far subject are sharp at the same time, with a hidden optical seam between them.

## Narrative Function

- Keeps a foreground and a background subject sharp simultaneously, visually giving them equal status.
- Effectively declares both planes equally present and equally real.
- The optical seam is usually hidden along a vertical edge or in shadow.

## How It Works

- Mount a close-up lens covering only half the front of the lens; that half acts like a magnifying glass.
- The uncovered side focuses on the distant subject, while the covered side renders the near object sharp.
- Hide the dividing line along an architectural edge or in a soft area.

## When to Use

- Use: When two subjects at different distances must both be clear, such as a listener and a distant figure, or a foreground object and background action.
- Avoid: Using it as a substitute for deep focus.
- Avoid: Using it to split the frame into two independent compositions.

## Film Examples

- *Blow Out* (1981): Eavesdropping equipment or faces sharp alongside distant action.
- *Jaws* (1975): Some scenes keep near and far characters in focus at once.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Split diopter shot in one continuous room: on the left, a ringing telephone in the extreme foreground is tack sharp, while on the right a man sitting far across the room is also perfectly sharp. A narrow soft blur band between them hides along a door frame, locked static camera.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confused with deep focus (seamless even sharpness) or split screen (two independent images), producing a harsh vertical cut line, ghosting, or even sharpness with no optical seam → create two distinct sharp zones within a continuous space, with only a narrow transitional blur band between them.
