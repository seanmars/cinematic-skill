# Magnification

Summary: The sense of scale deliberately disappears; a one-centimeter object is enlarged into an entire landscape, and tiny elements become complete dramatic subjects.

## Narrative Function

- Fills the frame with a tiny subject, deliberately making the audience lose their sense of scale.
- Turns a one-centimeter world into an entire landscape, letting microscopic elements carry full drama.

## How It Works

- First set the reproduction ratio (e.g. 1:2, 1:1, 2:1), then choose matching optics: macro lens, bellows, probe lens or microscope.
- Redesign the lighting for the new scale.
- At high magnification depth of field shrinks drastically, and camera vibration is magnified too.

## When to Use

- Use: When a collapse of scale beyond an ordinary close-up is truly needed.
- Avoid: Using it as shorthand for a tight portrait.
- Avoid: Simply digitally cropping and enlarging a wide image.

## Film Examples

- *Microcosmos* (1996): True high magnification, treating insects as complete dramatic protagonists.
- *Powers of Ten* (1977): The whole short is structured by jumping in and out of scale in powers of ten.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Extreme magnification at 2:1 reproduction ratio: the compound eye of a housefly fills the entire frame, each tiny lens facet visible like a honeycomb landscape. Collapsed depth of field, razor-sharp focus on a narrow slice, no sense of real-world scale.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Treating a facial extreme close-up as true magnification (the whole eye visible, depth of field still fairly normal) → reach a ratio near life-size or higher, rendering centimeter-scale terrain with razor-sharp focus.
