# 14mm Ultra-Wide

Summary: With the camera close to the subject, the foreground swells and the background recedes rapidly, making space feel aggressive.

## Narrative Function

- Close yet confrontational; puts the audience right up against the character while the surroundings feel oppressive and inescapable.
- Foreground scale balloons, the background recedes exaggeratedly, and space becomes invasive.
- Edge distortion is obvious, so it's unsuitable for flattering portraits.

## How It Works

- Place the camera about an arm's length from the subject, then frame with 14mm.
- Rectilinear optics keep lines straight, unlike a fisheye.
- Perspective exaggeration comes from "close distance", not just the focal length itself.
- Keep faces as close to the optical center as possible to avoid edge stretching.

## When to Use

- Use: When the subject must be very close and the surroundings should feel invasive and inescapable.
- Avoid: Using it as a default portrait lens, or a generic "wide angle".
- Avoid: When only moderate environmental context is needed, without heroic distortion.

## Film Examples

- *Fallen Angels* (1995): Faces pressed close to the lens, with an expanded city nightscape behind.
- *Fear and Loathing in Las Vegas* (1998): Extreme proximity amplifies subjective derangement.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Shot on a 14mm rectilinear ultra-wide lens from arm's length: a man's face looms large in the foreground while the neon-lit street behind him stretches away dramatically. Walls and poles stay straight, no fisheye curvature, exaggerated near-far scale, claustrophobic energy.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Lumping 14mm together with fisheye, or using 24mm from far away but labeling it 14mm → put the camera very close to the subject, keep lines mostly straight, and let foreground objects dominate in scale.
