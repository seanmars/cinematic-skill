# Macro

Summary: Near 1:1 magnification enlarges tiny objects to fill the frame, turning surface texture into an entire landscape.

## Narrative Function

- Makes a tiny subject the entire composition, turning details hard to see with the naked eye into dramatic focus.
- Depth of field collapses to extremely thin, and texture becomes landscape.

## How It Works

- Use a macro lens or close-focus gear, and secure the subject firmly.
- Light to emphasize surface relief: hard light reveals texture, soft wrapping light renders translucency.
- The focal plane is razor-thin; a tiny shift crosses a significant spatial distance.

## When to Use

- Use: When microscopic detail drives the narrative, such as insect behavior, mechanical structures, ritualistic repetition with small objects.
- Avoid: Confusing it with an ordinary close-up.
- Avoid: Enlarging an ordinary image in post and calling it macro.

## Film Examples

- *Requiem for a Dream* (2000): Extreme-detail images compress ritualistic behavior into sensory fragments.
- *Amélie* (2001): Texture-driven cinematography turns ordinary objects into tactile pleasures.
- *Microcosmos* (1996): Insects filmed as complete narrative drama.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Macro shot at 1:1 magnification: a single drop of dew on a leaf fills the entire frame, its surface reflecting the sky, the leaf's veins forming a vast textured terrain. Razor-thin focus plane, soft falloff, low raking light sculpting the surface.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Treating a portrait-lens extreme close-up or an enlarged crop as macro (skin pores at normal size, ample depth of field) → have the small object truly fill the sensor, keep real close-focus falloff, and light the surface as sculpted terrain.
