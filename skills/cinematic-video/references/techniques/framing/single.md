# Single

Summary: The frame belongs to one main character only; even if the other person is present, they are erased by the composition.

## Narrative Function

- Psychologically isolates a character within a conversation, giving them a private emotional beat.
- Removes the relational information a two-person composition provides, temporarily "deleting" the other person.

## How It Works

- One person occupies the whole frame.
- Clean single: no second body at the frame edge at all; dirty single: allows a little of the other person's edge or a blurred shoulder, but not enough to form an over-the-shoulder anchor.
- Common sizes are close-up or MCU; medium singles also exist.

## When to Use

- Use: Moments in a conversation needing introspection or emphasis on one character's private reaction.
- Avoid: Cutting to singles too early, making the relationship invisible.
- Avoid: Confusing it with the duration concept of a "oner" (one continuous take).

## Film Examples

- *The Silence of the Lambs* (1991): Interview passages use frontal clean singles, making the glass partition and the other person vanish psychologically, leaving only the facial performance.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Clean single of a woman in a medium close-up during a tense conversation, no second person or foreground shoulder anywhere in frame. She looks just off-lens toward her unseen partner, the frame belonging entirely to her.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing it with an OTS (a clear second face appears in frame) → A clean single must truly exclude any other framed body.
- Calling a one-take long shot a single → Describe "how many people are in frame" and "shot duration" separately.
