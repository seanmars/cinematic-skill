# Over-the-Shoulder Frame

Summary: Shoots past the blurred shoulder of one person in the foreground to the sharp face of the person opposite, keeping their relationship in frame throughout.

## Narrative Function

- The default framing for dialogue scenes, preserving both people's spatial relationship.
- The weight of the foreground shoulder can convey pressure, intimacy or entrapment.
- Even in a tight frame, the other person remains "present".

## How It Works

- Place the camera beside one person and shoot past their out-of-focus shoulder to the far person's sharp face.
- The far person looks at the near shoulder, never at the lens.
- Reverse shots stay on the same side of the axis (180-degree rule); foreground body position, head size and eyelines must match each other.

## When to Use

- Use: Conversations where relationship dynamics must be visible, such as interviews, confrontations, intimate scenes.
- Avoid: When a character needs to be isolated.
- Avoid: When both faces need to be equally clear and complete (use a two-shot).

## Film Examples

- *Heat* (1995): The diner conversation uses opposing over-the-shoulder coverage.
- *The Silence of the Lambs* (1991): Interrogation-style interviews.
- *The Stendhal Syndrome* (1996): Alternating viewpoints link the character with the paintings.
- *The Social Network* (2010): Combative deposition scenes.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Over-the-shoulder shot from behind a detective's soft, out-of-focus left shoulder, framing the suspect's sharp face across the interrogation table. The suspect looks at the detective, not the lens; plan a matching reverse on the same side of the 180-degree line with identical lighting.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Both faces equally sharp, or no foreground shoulder visible at all → Keep one soft-focus shoulder + one sharp face looking at it.
- Crossing the line, with the far person looking at the lens or the wrong direction → Stay on the same side of the axis for reverses.
