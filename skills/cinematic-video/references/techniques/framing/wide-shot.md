# Wide Shot

Summary: The place is the subject of the frame; figures are visible but secondary, and the spatial geography reads clearly.

## Narrative Function

- Conveys spatial relationships and geographic context: which doors lead out, how far the road goes, what the weather is.
- Avoids isolating characters too early.

## How It Works

- Architecture, streets or weather stay readable, with the body taking up less height than in a full-body portrait.
- Focal length isn't fixed (24mm indoors, longer lenses in open spaces); the key is that the environment is still "speaking".

## When to Use

- Use: When spatial geography needs to keep carrying weight and characters shouldn't be isolated yet.
- Avoid: When the dramatic focus is facial detail or a close view of costume.
- Avoid: Figures so small they can't be seen.

## Film Examples

- *Lawrence of Arabia* (1962): Geography is ongoing coverage, not backdrop.
- *Once Upon a Time in the West* (1968): The station and desert overwhelm the diminished figures.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Wide shot of a rain-soaked night market street, stalls, awnings and the alley exits all clearly readable, three small figures weaving between umbrellas in the mid-ground. 24mm lens, the place itself is the subject, people remain visible but secondary.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Shooting a full-body shot where the figure fills the height, or mislabeling a long shot as a wide → Keep the place as the subject, with figures small enough to maintain the "map" function.
