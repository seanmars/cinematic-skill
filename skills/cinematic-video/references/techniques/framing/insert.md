# Insert Shot

Summary: A close shot of a story-critical object, hand, text or screen, conveying facts a wide shot can't show clearly.

## Narrative Function

- Provides information essential to moving the story forward that a wide shot can't convey.
- Must serve the story, not decorate it.
- It describes a "function", not a shot size.

## How It Works

- Get tight on the object, but keep space and lighting consistent with the scene it belongs to.
- Use macro only for truly tiny details; 50mm is enough for a letter-sized object.
- Keep hands at natural scale, and don't add extra light on the object's surface.

## When to Use

- Use: Details that will become crucial later, such as keys, a trembling water glass, an envelope.
- Avoid: Purely decorative objects, or catalog-style glamorous lighting.
- Avoid: When fully leaving the main action (that is a cutaway).

## Film Examples

- *Citizen Kane* (1941): The word on the sled reveals the theme.
- *Notorious* (1946): A hidden key becomes crucial.
- *Rear Window* (1954): Details of optical equipment advance the plot.
- *Jurassic Park* (1993): Ripples in a water glass foreshadow the approaching threat.
- *Zodiac* (2007): Handwriting and documents drive the narrative.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Insert shot of a trembling hand sliding a folded note under a coffee cup on a diner counter, hand at natural scale, lit by the same warm overhead practicals as the room. Static camera, the only motion is the slight shake of the fingers.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing it with a cutaway (leaving the scene) → An insert must stay within the current action.
- Shooting a story object with reflective product-ad lighting → Keep composition and light within the scene's own spatial and tonal logic, with a story reason.
