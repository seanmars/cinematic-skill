# Cut-In

Summary: Tightens from a wide shot onto a detail already present in the frame, without changing spatial orientation.

## Narrative Function

- Gives visual proof of a detail the master implies but doesn't show clearly (hand, phone, wound).
- Because the detail is already part of the scene's geography, the audience doesn't lose orientation.

## How It Works

- Cuts from a wider framing to a closer shot of an object or action already established in the same space.
- Usually keeps a similar camera axis and screen direction.
- Similar to an axial cut, but the emphasis is on the closer shot it "lands" on, not the cut's jump itself.

## When to Use

- Use: Emphasizing an important small detail within an established scene.
- Avoid: When the object never appeared in the master.
- Avoid: Turning to a completely different position or another wall.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Insert](insert.md) | Introduces a new story object; a cut-in tightens onto an existing element |
| [Axial Cut](../editing/axial-cut.md) | A cut that jumps along the lens axis; a cut-in is the tighter shot it lands on |
| [Extreme Close-Up](extreme-close-up.md) | Describes frame size, not a coverage transition |

## Film Examples

- Classical Hollywood coverage: shoot the master first, then tighten onto the action details it implies.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Cut-in from a wide office shot to a tighter frame of the same desk seen earlier, now showing the manager's hand hovering over a ringing phone. Same camera axis and screen direction as the wide, lighting and props identical to the master.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Cutting to an entirely new space, or showing props absent from the master (that becomes a cutaway or insert) → Maintain spatial continuity and a consistent axis.
