# Extreme Close-Up

Summary: The frame holds only a single part of a face or object (eye, mouth, fingertip); the detail itself is the event of the scene.

## Narrative Function

- Strips away all environmental context, enlarging a single detail into the story's protagonist.
- Turns "looking" into a confrontation or pressure the audience cannot escape.
- If the actor needs the whole face to convey emotion, this shot size isn't suitable.

## How It Works

- The composition holds only one anatomical feature, with the frame edges cutting inside the facial outline.
- A longer lens (about 75-100mm) keeps facial proportions undistorted better than a wide-angle.
- Shallow depth of field, a macro lens and tight composition are three independent choices and needn't be combined; lighting decides whether to emphasize pore texture.

## When to Use

- Use: Moments where the detail is the point, such as a decision in the eyes or silent lips.
- Avoid: Ordinary dialogue, beauty portraits, or passages needing shoulder or body information.
- Cut away before the texture starts to distract from viewing.

## Film Examples

- *The Passion of Joan of Arc* (1928): Facial details under the pressure of interrogation.
- *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly* (1966): The pre-duel montage centers on eyes; the stare itself is the shootout.
- *Persona* (1966): Faces so close their identities seem to merge.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Extreme close-up of a single human eye filling the entire frame, iris and lashes in sharp detail, skin texture visible. Locked-off camera on a long lens, the only motion is a slow blink and a slight flick of the pupil.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Shooting it as a choker or close-up (hairline and chin visible, even shoulders) → Specify a single feature explicitly and crop inside the facial outline.
- Using a wide-angle to get close, causing distortion → Use a longer focal length; use macro only for very small objects.
