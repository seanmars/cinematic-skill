# Hyper-Saturation

Summary: Correct hues are pushed to an intensity far beyond reality, making the image look lacquered or like a fever dream.

## Narrative Function

- Uses color to convey emotion rather than record reality; colors become vivid, intense, almost operatic.
- Suits psychological, fantastical narratives rather than realist statements.

## How It Works

- Push saturation up, especially primaries, while minding how skin tones are handled.
- Control contrast and saturation separately.
- Production design must supply strong base colors first, then the grade amplifies them; skin tones can be protected or pushed along.

## When to Use

- Use: Scenes where emotional expression takes priority over realism, e.g. nightmares, combat, romance.
- Avoid: Documentary or situations claiming authenticity.
- Avoid: Controlled complementary palettes that need believable skin tones.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Teal and Orange](teal-orange.md) | Controlled complementary pairing with protected skin, not unlimited color boost |
| [Cross Process](cross-process.md) | Hues are skewed (cyan shadows, magenta skin), not correct primaries intensified |
| [Giallo](../genre-looks/giallo-look.md) | A complete genre style package, often including hyper-saturation |
| [Desaturation](desaturation.md) | The exact opposite direction |

## Film Examples

- *Suspiria* (1977): Reds and blues at maximum intensity; color acts like a physical force.
- *In the Mood for Love* (2000): Intense colors of corridors and fabrics exceed natural intensity.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Hyper-saturated color: deep lacquer reds, electric blues and rich emerald greens pushed far beyond realism, like a fever dream. Hues stay true to themselves, just louder; bold set colors and costumes, contrast kept separate, no documentary look.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confused with teal-orange or cross-process → keep hues correct and only raise saturation.
- The scene itself has no strong color and the grade alone forces it → let production design provide the color foundation.
