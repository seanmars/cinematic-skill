# Rack Focus

Summary: Without a cut or camera move, focus slides from one subject to another, and the audience's attention shifts with it.

## Narrative Function

- Guides the audience's eye with the focal plane instead of a cut.
- A slow rack reads like a narrative sentence; a fast snap is an emphatic hit.
- Suited to revealing the relationship between foreground and background subjects, such as pursuer and pursued.

## How It Works

- Mark the two distance planes in advance and rehearse the timing with the focus puller.
- Keep the camera still, or coordinate subtle movement with the rack.
- A wide aperture makes the effect more pronounced; lens breathing may shift the framing slightly, so decide whether to keep it deliberately.

## When to Use

- Use: When information must be revealed or attention shifted without a cut.
- Avoid: When both planes must be sharp at the same time.
- Avoid: When a direct cut would be stronger, or the scene needs sustained deep focus.

## Film Examples

- *Peking Opera Blues* (1986): Focus switches between the hunted and the pursuing police.
- *The Graduate* (1967): Selective focus isolates Benjamin from the surrounding crowd.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Locked-off shot with a very shallow depth of field: a letter on the desk in the foreground starts sharp while a man standing in the doorway behind it is a soft blur. Over about two seconds the focus pulls smoothly to the man, the letter melting into blur as he becomes crisp. No zoom, no cut, framing unchanged.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Unwanted cuts, warping effects, or both planes sharp → keep the composition unchanged as one plane softens while the other sharpens.
- Subject moves or deforms during the rack → keep the subject stable and move only the focus.
