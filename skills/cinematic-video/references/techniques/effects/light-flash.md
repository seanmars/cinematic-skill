# Light Flash

Summary: The frame instantly overexposes to pure white, then slowly recovers; the intense light itself becomes the impact and the transition.

## Narrative Function

- Creates impact with a brief overexposure that is both a cut point and an emotional "wound."
- Conveys a memory flashback, an explosion or a moment of trauma.
- The white peak can hide the cut.

## How It Works

- Overexpose a practical light, flash or muzzle flash until the frame clips to full white.
- Can be done in-camera (camera flash, daylight flooding in through an opening door) or in post-production.
- A thin haze can linger as the image recovers; cutting at the white peak works best.

## When to Use

- Use: Abrupt transitions between scenes or memories.
- Use: Combat or shock moments.
- Avoid: As a decorative lens artifact on a static shot; use lens flare for a sustained glow.

## Film Examples

- *Memento* (2000): White flashes link Polaroid photos with present-day memory.
- *Saving Private Ryan* (1998): Muzzle flashes overexpose the frame, punctuating the combat.
- *Raging Bull* (1980): Ringside photographers' flashbulbs fire constantly.

## Prompt Examples

```text
A camera flashbulb fires in the face of a boxer, blowing the entire frame out to pure white for four frames; as the exposure settles back, a faint milky veil lingers before the image returns to normal contrast in a different moment of the fight. The white peak hides the cut; no lingering lens streak.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Becomes a sustained lens streak, or just a hard cut with no exposure event → clip to white for a few frames, then recover.
- The cut point shows → hide the cut in the white peak.
