# Smash and Grab

Summary: A handheld camera is pressed into a chaotic scene, glass sprays toward the lens, and the grab is already escaping as it happens.

## Narrative Function

- Conveys the urgency of "a moment being stolen," for robberies, paparazzi and panic scenes.
- The camera is inside the action, as if arriving "too close and too late."
- Creates impact and chaos rather than pre-choreographed action.

## How It Works

- Handheld with a short focal length lens, making glass a foreground element flying toward the camera.
- A single moment of impact, immediately followed by the camera chasing the escape.
- Motion blur is acceptable, but avoid centered composition.
- A hard flash or strobe can freeze debris; keep the stolen object or broken window in frame.

## When to Use

- Use: Cutting into a scene mid-theft or mid-chaos.
- Avoid: Fight exchanges that need clear spatial relationships.
- Avoid: Quiet, planned standoffs or documentary-style interviews.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |

## Film Examples

- *Uncut Gems* (2019): Tense close handheld, glare and urgency.
- *The Town* (2010): Actual smash-and-grab robbery shots of shattering glass and grabbing.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Frantic handheld close shot with a wide short lens: a gloved fist smashes a jewelry store window and glass shards burst straight toward the camera, frozen for an instant by a hard flash. A hand snatches a necklace and the camera lurches after the thief already running, framing off-center and smeared with motion blur.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Action looks staged, composition centered, store interior clean and spacious → keep messy frame edges and let debris fly toward the lens.
- Subject lingers in frame → have the subject already leaving, with the camera chasing.
