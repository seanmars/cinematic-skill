# Vignette

Summary: The corners of the frame darken naturally, gently drawing the eye to the center.

## Narrative Function

- Pulls the audience's attention to the center of the frame without a harsh spotlight feel.
- Simulates vintage lens character, acknowledging that off-axis areas are naturally darker.
- Keep it subtle so edge information stays readable.

## How It Works

- Cause: vintage lenses wide open, wide-angle lenses with insufficient coverage, or filters / ND grads.
- Optically follows cosine-fourth natural falloff; lens barrel obstruction produces mechanical vignetting.
- In post-production, darken the corners with a soft gradient that follows the lens shape, avoiding harsh geometric shapes.

## When to Use

- Use: Scenes where the corners are naturally a stop darker, such as blue-hour rooftops or candlelit interiors.
- Avoid: Heavy vignetting on group shots or extreme wide shots that makes edge characters unreadable.
- Avoid: Applying it across the board like a social media filter.

## Film Examples

- *Barry Lyndon* (1975): Fast vintage lenses show natural corner falloff in candlelit interiors.
- *The Witch* (2015): Quiet edge falloff from vintage lenses keeps attention in the forest clearing and the kitchen.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Candlelit farmhouse interior filmed on a vintage lens wide open: the corners fall off gently by about one stop, a soft organic roll-off that pulls the eye toward the family at the table in the center. Objects near the frame edges remain faintly readable; no hard oval mask.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A perfect circular crop or a heavy post-production oval unrelated to lens character → use soft falloff measured in stops.
- Vignette crushes subjects at the edges → keep edge information and stay consistent with the optical source.
