# Bokeh

Summary: Out-of-focus highlights become soft aperture discs, the background turns into pure light, and the subject stands out sharply.

## Narrative Function

- The focus is on the "aesthetic quality" of the out-of-focus area: disc shape, edge character and smoothness, not just shallow depth of field.
- Turns background information into a texture of light, isolating the subject from the environment.
- Creates a romantic, lonely or dreamlike mood.

## How It Works

- Open the aperture wide to separate subject from background.
- The lens determines disc shape: round for spherical, oval for anamorphic, and mechanical vignetting at the frame edges produces cat's-eye shapes.
- Point sources (practicals, specular highlights) reveal bokeh best; distance and focal length control disc size.

## When to Use

- Use: Close portraits in tight spaces, where background practicals and neon turn into points of light.
- Avoid: Wide shots that need readable blocking.
- Avoid: When the background itself carries narrative information.

## Film Examples

- *In the Mood for Love* (2000): Hong Kong streetlights become soft discs beside the characters' faces.
- *Her* (2013): City lights behind Theodore melt through the lens optics, not digital smearing.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Close-up portrait of a woman on a rainy balcony at night, shot wide open on a fast 85mm lens: her eyes are tack sharp while distant street lamps and neon signs melt into large, smooth circular bokeh discs with soft edges. Natural optical falloff between planes, no cutout look.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Using a cutout matte or generic blur with no aperture disc structure → keep optical bokeh with its edge character.
- Background looks replaced rather than optically defocused → maintain spatial separation between foreground and background.
