# Forced Perspective

Summary: Camera position and set geometry make people at different distances look like a giant and a small person sharing the same space.

## Narrative Function

- Creates size differences through physical placement rather than digital scaling.
- Works only from a single locked-off viewpoint; once the camera moves, the illusion breaks.
- Lets characters of different scales share the frame and interact naturally.

## How It Works

- Decide the camera position first, then place subjects at different distances.
- Scale props and sets according to each actor's distance from the lens.
- Align eyelines and foot contact points, and keep lighting consistent across both scales.
- Lock off the camera, or move it only along a path that preserves alignment.

## When to Use

- Use: When two characters of different scales must share a space, such as a giant and a hobbit at the same table.
- Avoid: When the camera must move off-axis or orbit the subject.
- Avoid: When a real miniature set has already been built, or a shallow depth of field is needed.

## Film Examples

- *The Lord of the Rings: The Fellowship of the Ring* (2001): Distance and scaled props put humans and hobbits in the same frame.
- *Eternal Sunshine of the Spotless Mind* (2004): Adult characters look like children in an enlarged childhood room.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Locked-off wide shot of a farmhouse kitchen table: a towering wizard sits at the near end while a tiny hobbit sits at the far end, both appearing to share the same tabletop and ground plane. Their eyelines meet naturally, contact shadows are scaled to each figure, and everything stays sharp in deep focus. No camera movement, no digital resizing.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Feet seem to float, eyelines don't match → align contact points and eyelines, and scale shadows proportionally.
- Blur reveals true distances → keep enough depth of field.
- Camera orbits freely and breaks the illusion → lock to a single axis and keep lighting consistent.
