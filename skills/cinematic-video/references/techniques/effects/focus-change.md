# Focus Change

Summary: Without cutting, focus "snaps" to a new subject, decisively redirecting the audience's attention.

## Narrative Function

- Decisively shifts attention to a new focal point.
- Used for moments when a new subject must instantly dominate the frame.
- Unsuited to slow, exploratory focus pulls or situations needing multiple planes sharp at once.

## How It Works

- Lock off the camera or keep it nearly still, with a shallow depth of field.
- The follow focus jumps or hard-slides quickly between two distance marks.
- The new subject ends sharp and crisp; 75-100mm is most effective, while wide angles weaken the impact.
- Keep the blur in between short.

## When to Use

- Use: Sudden emphasis on a clue, a weapon or an eavesdropper.
- Avoid: When you want the audience to watch focus move slowly, or both subjects must be sharp.
- Avoid: Slow multi-plane searches or drawn-out blur transitions.

## Film Examples

- *The Graduate* (1967): Decisive focus handoffs establish a grammar for shifting attention.
- *There Will Be Blood* (2007): Interior scenes swap sharpness rapidly within fixed compositions.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Locked 85mm shot with very shallow depth of field: a detective in the foreground is sharp, the doorway behind him a soft blur. Suddenly the focus snaps hard to the doorway, where a figure is listening, the detective dissolving into blur in a split second as the new subject lands crisp.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Turning into a slow focal shift, or just a hard cut → use a shallow depth of field and complete the change with a fast snap or hard slide.
- Blur transition drags on and smears together → shorten the blur in between; the new subject must be sharp immediately.
