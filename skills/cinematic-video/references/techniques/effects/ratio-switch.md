# Ratio Switch

Summary: The frame's aspect ratio changes within the shot, so the frame itself expands or narrows and becomes part of the story.

## Narrative Function

- Makes the frame a character, conveying confinement, freedom or a story turn.
- The outer frame opening or closing marks a shift in time, point of view or possibility.
- Not a zoom effect, and not an in-frame mask.

## How It Works

- Compose with safe areas for both ratios.
- Use a dynamic crop or side mattes to reveal or hide previously unseen areas.
- Keep the subject's geometry unchanged; people must never be stretched to fill the new frame.
- The camera can stay locked off; the event is the change of the border.

## When to Use

- Use: The moment a character "pushes" the world open, or when a new period needs a different frame shape.
- Avoid: As a zoom out that only changes shot size.
- Avoid: Using a lens zoom to fake a wider frame.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Masking](masking.md) | Cuts a shape within a stable outer frame; a ratio switch changes the outer frame itself |
| [Wide Shot](../framing/wide-shot.md) | Uses a wider shot within the existing ratio; a ratio switch changes the container |

## Film Examples

- *Mommy* (2014): The character pushes the 1:1 frame wider, symbolizing a brief liberation.
- *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (2014): Different eras of the story use different aspect ratios.

## Prompt Examples

```text
A boy rides a bicycle down a sunny street inside a narrow square 1:1 frame; mid-shot, the black side bars slide outward and the image opens to a full 2.39:1 widescreen, revealing the rest of the street. Proportions stay exact, nothing is stretched, and the camera does not zoom.
```

## Common Mistakes

- People are stretched wider or fatter to fill the new frame → keep the original geometry and only reveal more of the image.
- Only a lens zoom with no matte movement → make the outer frame truly change by cropping / revealing.
