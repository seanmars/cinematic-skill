# Collage

Summary: Build the image from visibly cut or torn fragments (different film stocks, paper, live action), deliberately leaving the seams visible.

## Narrative Function

- Deliberately exposes the traces of "being assembled" rather than blending seamlessly.
- Lets multiple sources keep their own identity, creating satire, scrapbook-style memory or a graphic-design feel.
- Rejects photographic unity and emphasizes the author's hand.

## How It Works

- Collect or shoot material, cut it out, and composite it with hard-edged or torn-edge mattes.
- Keep paper fibers, inconsistent grain and visible borders.
- Do not retouch all elements into smooth uniformity.

## When to Use

- Use: Title sequences, satirical segments, scrapbook-like memories, or graphic segments that reject photographic unity.
- Avoid: When two photos should blend by density (use double exposure instead).
- Avoid: When live action and animation need to share space without torn edges.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Double Exposure](double-exposure.md) | Optical or additive layering, relying on translucency rather than scissor marks |
| [Mixed Media](mixed-media.md) | The broader animation-plus-live-action family; collage is its cut-and-paste grammar |
| [Split Screen](../editing/split-screen.md) | Divides multiple images simultaneously with designed borders, usually live action; collage ignores a shared timeline |

## Film Examples

- Terry Gilliam's cut-out collage animation for *Monty Python's Flying Circus* (1969).
- *The Jinx* (2015) title sequence: Treats documents and images as stacked flat layers.
- Bruce Conner's *A Movie* (1958): Uses found-footage collage as film grammar.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Animated collage title sequence: a black-and-white live-action man walks across a torn sheet of yellowed newspaper, next to a cut-out Victorian engraving of a hot-air balloon and a strip of saturated 1970s color film. Ragged paper fibers, mismatched grain, and hard scissor edges stay clearly visible; no smooth translucent blending.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Turning into translucent blending, uniform grain, or a clean split screen with no torn paper → use different film textures and torn or cut edges.
- Seams retouched too smoothly → present it as flat layers that emphasize construction.
