# Lens Flare

Summary: A strong light source in frame or near the frame edge scatters inside the lens into flare spots, ghosts and an overall haze, lowering image contrast.

## Narrative Function

- Conveys the moment when "the light is too strong for the camera to handle," not a decorative effect.
- Aperture shapes repeat across the frame as ghosts, implying the overwhelming presence of the light source.
- Suited to conveying being engulfed by light, awe, redemption or the arrival of an unknown force.

## How It Works

- Place a small, hard light source (sun, HMI, bare bulb) in frame or slightly off the optical axis.
- Uncoated or vintage lenses increase scatter; a dirty front element produces full-frame veiling glare.
- Spherical lenses produce circular ghosts; anamorphic cylindrical elements produce horizontal streaks.

## When to Use

- Use: Opening a door into glaring daylight, headlights approaching in the dark, a window facing a bright exterior.
- Use: Moments when the light source itself is the story's focus.
- Avoid: When no light source in frame can explain the flare.

## Film Examples

- *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977): The alien craft's intense light overwhelms the camera's optics.
- *Star Trek* (2009): Bright practical lights on the bridge constantly throw streaks and haze.

## Prompt Examples

```text
A bare tungsten bulb sits just inside the upper-left corner of frame, scattering light through the lens: a soft veiling haze lowers contrast and a chain of faint hexagonal ghosts trails diagonally across the image. When a character walks past and blocks the bulb, the flare vanishes instantly.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Flare looks like an overlay floating on the image with no matching in-frame source → anchor the flare to an actual light.
- Flare persists when the source is blocked → the flare must move with the source and disappear when blocked.
