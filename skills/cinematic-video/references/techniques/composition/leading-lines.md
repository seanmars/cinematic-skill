# Leading Lines

Summary: Use real lines such as rails, roads, corridors or shadows to lead the eye to (or through) the subject.

## Narrative Function

- Lines are visual vectors that decide where attention flows.
- When lines converge on a character, the character is the destination; when lines continue past, the character becomes a gateway into deeper space.
- The technique fails when the scene has no real directional geometry.

## How It Works

- First find real architectural or natural edges in the environment, then adjust the camera position so lines converge on the focal point.
- A wide lens (24-35mm) strengthens convergence; a long lens flattens lines and weakens their direction.
- Keep the horizon level unless the lines themselves are diagonal.

## When to Use

- Use: scenes with inherent directional lines, such as corridors, highways, stations, church naves.
- Avoid: forcing convergence lines in featureless spaces.
- Avoid: close-ups that only need to focus on the face.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Vanishing Point](vanishing-point.md) | Parallel lines meet within the frame; leading lines don't have to meet in frame |
| [One-Point Perspective](one-point-perspective.md) | A complete axial structure with one dominant vanishing point; leading lines work with a single edge in a cluttered environment |
| [Diagonal Composition](diagonal-composition.md) | Uses diagonal energy, no convergence needed |
| [Architexture](architexture.md) | Material fills the frame; direction is secondary |

## Film Examples

- *The Shining* (1980): Carpets, corridors and the tricycle's path lead the eye through the space.
- *The Deer Hunter* (1978): Steel structures, roads and industrial edges push focus deep into the working environment.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Leading lines composition: two steel rails and the platform edge of an empty train station converge toward a lone figure standing at the far end. 28mm wide lens, level horizon, cool morning haze, the lines clearly terminating on the subject.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Architectural lines placed randomly behind a centered portrait without actually pointing at the subject → move the camera until the lines truly aim at the subject, or drop the technique and shoot a clean portrait.
