# Voyeur

Summary: The audience peeks from behind cover while the subject is completely unaware, giving the frame a transgressive tension.

## Narrative Function

- Places the audience in a hidden position, effectively "trespassing."
- The subject doesn't know they are being watched; tension comes from the unauthorized point of view, not performed intimacy.

## How It Works

- Place physical cover between lens and subject (foliage, glass, grilles).
- Open the aperture enough to soften the cover while keeping the subject sharp.
- A long lens helps create the necessary distance.
- Don't put the cover dead center unless it is deliberately designed as an iris-like mask.

## When to Use

- Use: when watching is itself a transgression, e.g. affairs, surveillance, hunting.
- Avoid: decorative use in scenes performed for the camera.
- Avoid: confusing it with close-ups where the character is aware of the camera.

## Film Examples

- *Rear Window* (1954): Observing the courtyard becomes the plot itself.
- *The Conversation* (1974): Wiretapping and surveillance gradually erode the notion of privacy.
- *Caché* (2005): A distant, unauthorized view with an aggressively present camera.
- *M* (1931): Off-screen sound implies a presence beyond the frame.
- *The Blair Witch Project* (1999): Night sounds suggest movement outside the tent.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Voyeur shot: seen from behind dense hedge leaves across the street, a telephoto lens watches a couple arguing inside a lit living room window, completely unaware of being observed. Soft out-of-focus foliage along the edges, no eye contact with the camera, tense secretive mood.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Mistaking a decorative dirty frame or ordinary dialogue coverage for voyeur → use real cover, a plausible hiding position, and no eye contact between subject and lens.
