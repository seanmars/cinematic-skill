# Dirty Frame

Summary: The frame edges are occupied by out-of-focus shoulders, glass, foliage or crowds, like a scene glimpsed in secret.

## Narrative Function

- Occupied edges suggest an unauthorized or covertly filmed point of view.
- Out-of-focus foreground occlusion conveys surveillance, crowding, or being in a space that isn't ready for you.
- Contrasts with clean, isolated compositions.

## How It Works

- Place objects around the frame periphery and use a wide aperture to throw them out of focus (unless a deliberately sharp intrusion is wanted).
- A long lens compresses background elements into frame; a wide lens needs objects very close to the lens.
- Keep a clear central area for the subject.

## When to Use

- Use: voyeuristic situations such as parties, streets, surveillance, trespassing.
- Avoid: formal portraits, product shots, ceremonial scenes.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

## Film Examples

- *The Conversation* (1974): Watching through crowds and glass, privacy is violated.
- *Children of Men* (2006): Rubble and close bodies keep the frame cluttered and the future uncertain.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Dirty frame: shot through a crowded party, blurry shoulders and a glass edge occupy the left and right borders of the frame, while two people whisper in sharp focus in the clear center. Long lens, shallow depth of field, candid surveillance feeling.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Accidental clutter or fake vignetting passed off as the technique → deliberately choose occluders, place them at the edges out of focus, and don't block the subject's mouth.
