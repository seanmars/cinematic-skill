# Look Space

Summary: Leave space in the direction of the character's gaze so the unseen off-screen target commands attention.

## Narrative Function

- Space ahead of the gaze creates tension, letting the off-screen target dominate attention even without appearing.
- This "unused air" only truly matters when the key thing is outside the frame.

## How It Works

- Leave more frame in the gaze direction than behind the head.
- Align the height and direction of the gaze with the next shot showing the target (eyeline match).
- Profiles or three-quarter profiles best show the space the gaze "owns."

## When to Use

- Use: when the object of attention is off-screen or about to enter, e.g. observers, painters, characters waiting for someone.
- Avoid: when the character addresses the camera directly, or when the space has no narrative role in an interview.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Lead Room](lead-room.md) | Emphasizes direction of movement; look space deals only with the gaze itself |
| [Point of View](../camera-angles/pov.md) | Shows what the character sees; look space shows the character "looking" |
| [Short Siding](short-siding.md) | Deliberately removes this space |
| Reaction Shot | If the face carries all the drama, the space can be minimal |

## Film Examples

- *Rear Window* (1954): Jeff's gaze shots always precede the courtyard views.
- *The Stendhal Syndrome* (1996): Anna's gaze connects with paintings in the museum.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Look space framing: a woman in three-quarter profile sits at the far left of the frame, gazing intently toward the right, where a wide stretch of empty, softly lit room holds the tension of something unseen off-screen. 50mm lens, quiet suspense.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Extra space on the wrong side, or gaze directions that don't match across shots → put the extra frame on the gaze side; when completing an eyeline across a cut, keep the geometry consistent.
