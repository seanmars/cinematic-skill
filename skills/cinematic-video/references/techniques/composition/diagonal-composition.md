# Diagonal Composition

Summary: The camera stays level while diagonals in the scene (stairs, shadows, crowds) cut across the frame, creating momentum and unease.

## Narrative Function

- Replaces stable horizontals with diagonal lines to bring energy, motion, chaos or turmoil.
- Not suited to formal, orderly or solemn scenes.

## How It Works

- Keep the camera level; diagonals come from real architecture or blocking (stairs, shadows, bodies).
- A wide lens strengthens the thrust of diagonals into depth.
- Hard light can cast diagonal shadows to reinforce the effect.

## When to Use

- Use: action scenes, staircases, street violence, harsh weather, chases.
- Avoid: as default decoration for calm interiors.
- Avoid: faking it by tilting the camera when the scene has no diagonals.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Dutch Angle](../camera-angles/dutch-angle.md) | Rotates the camera so the horizon itself tilts |
| [Leading Lines](leading-lines.md) | Lines can be diagonal or straight; the point is guidance, not instability |
| [Rule of Thirds](rule-of-thirds.md) | A default balanced composition, unrelated to unease |

## Film Examples

- *Battleship Potemkin* (1925): Rifles, steps and crowds slash diagonally across the frame, giving the composition no rest.
- *The Third Man* (1949): Vienna streets and wet shadows form diagonal traps while the camera stays level.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Diagonal composition with a level camera: a steep stone staircase cuts from the lower-left to the upper-right corner as a crowd rushes down it, hard low sunlight casting long diagonal shadows across the steps. 24mm lens, chaotic energy, horizon kept straight.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confusing it with Dutch Angle: only the horizon tilts, with no real architectural diagonals in frame → level the camera and use diagonals that actually exist in the scene.
