# Central Framing

Summary: The subject faces the lens dead on the optical axis with equal space on both sides, as if placed on a display stand.

## Narrative Function

- Makes the character the focal point rather than part of the environment, with a formal, presentational tone.
- Equal space on both sides lends authority or reverence, and often brings deadpan humor.
- Not suited to moments where the character actively chases, flees or observes.

## How It Works

- Aim the optical axis at the subject's sternum or nose bridge; camera frontal, level and plumb.
- A long lens (65-85mm) emphasizes isolation; a wide lens brings in more environment.
- Check that the space on both sides is equal.

## When to Use

- Use: entrances, portraits, judgments, formal scenes with a comedic edge, moments when a character puts on a uniform or costume.
- Avoid: when the character needs lead room.
- Avoid: when the character should feel like a minor part of the environment.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Centered Composition](centered-composition.md) | The whole frame is organized on the central axis; Central Framing only governs subject placement |
| [Symmetry](symmetry.md) | The environment mirrors left and right; with a centered subject the environment can still be asymmetrical |
| [Rule of Thirds](rule-of-thirds.md) | The default composition being deliberately broken |

## Film Examples

- *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (2014): Concierge and guests framed frontally at center, formal yet humorous.
- *Moonrise Kingdom* (2012): Uniformed children shot frontally at center.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Dead-center framing: a hotel concierge faces the camera directly, placed exactly on the vertical center line with equal empty space on both sides. Frontal, level 75mm shot, formal and slightly deadpan, like a portrait on display.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Accidentally drifting toward a third line, with unequal side space → move the subject until both sides match; if you want an offset, switch explicitly to lead room.
- Camera slightly tilted or angled → keep it frontal and level.
