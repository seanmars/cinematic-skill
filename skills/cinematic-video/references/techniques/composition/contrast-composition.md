# Tonal Contrast

Summary: The brightest and darkest masses confront each other head-on, giving the frame poster-like graphic impact so the subject cannot be missed.

## Narrative Function

- Turns the frame into a high-impact graphic, favoring poster-like clarity over midtone detail.
- Suits good-versus-evil dualities and the intense mood of night scenes.
- Not suited to scenes that need subtle skin tones, food texture or mixed-light dialogue.

## How It Works

- Expose for the dominant tonal mass and let the other end fall to black or blow out.
- Use hard directional light and flags to control exactly where light lands.
- A dark subject needs a rim light or backlight to read; when the subject is the dark mass, remove fill.

## When to Use

- Use: night scenes, moral dualities, graphic title sequences, shots that must read from a distance.
- Avoid: scenes whose information depends on midtones or detail.

## Film Examples

- *Sin City* (2005): Value-first composition as narrative grammar.
- *The Lighthouse* (2019): Stark black-and-white tonal contrast as the core style.

## Prompt Examples

```text
High tonal contrast composition: a figure in a pure white coat stands in a pitch-black alley, lit by a single hard streetlight from above. No midtones, graphic poster-like black and white separation, crisp edges, dramatic noir mood.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Face, wall and clothes all in murky midtones → make the subject the brightest or darkest pole, and push everything else away with flags, exposure or wardrobe.
