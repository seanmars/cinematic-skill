# Short Siding

Summary: The face is pushed to the frame edge it looks toward, with a large space left behind the head, so the character seems to have nowhere to go.

## Narrative Function

- Strips the character of visual freedom, conveying entrapment, denial or despair.
- Wasted space behind the head implies the character can neither escape nor move forward.

## How It Works

- Put the eyes near the edge the character faces, leaving the larger area behind the head.
- Works with profiles or three-quarter profiles.
- The effect comes from the crop itself; a wider lens isn't necessarily needed.
- Don't "correct" it back when the camera pans.

## When to Use

- Use: interrogations, guilt, panic, or moments when progress seems out of reach.
- Avoid: as a default portrait composition; use it deliberately only when emotional pressure is needed.

## Film Examples

- *The Silence of the Lambs* (1991): Even in medium shots, short siding makes conversations feel caged.
- *Prisoners* (2013): Heads pressed to the frame edge with large empty space behind.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Short-sided framing: a suspect in profile looks toward the right edge of the frame, his nose almost touching the border, while a large empty gray wall fills the space behind his head. Tight medium close-up, cold fluorescent light, trapped and anxious mood.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A rushed pan that falls short, leaving faces against the edge in dialogue shots that aren't about entrapment → use lead room normally; hold this squeeze deliberately only in scenes that need pressure.
