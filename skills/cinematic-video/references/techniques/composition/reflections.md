# Reflections

Summary: Through mirrors, glass or water, the watcher and the watched, two spaces, appear in one frame at once.

## Narrative Function

- Shows two spaces simultaneously without cutting.
- Expresses inner division, double identity, or unequal dialogue relationships.
- Don't use it when the reflection is murky or violates physics.

## How It Works

- Plan the optical path before lighting and control the brightness difference so the reflection reads on the glass.
- Decide deliberately whether the camera is hidden or visible.
- The geometry between eyelines and reflections must be correct.
- Use a polarizer to control or eliminate reflections; on water, adjust the shooting angle.

## When to Use

- Use: split identities, conversations that can't happen face to face, cityscapes reflected in windows, parent-child relationships shown through mirrors.
- Avoid: using reflective surfaces as set dressing with no narrative purpose.

## Film Examples

- *Paris, Texas* (1984): An unequal conversation in a one-way-mirror booth.
- *The Lady from Shanghai* (1947): A hall of mirrors endlessly duplicates the characters' positions.
- *Contact* (1997): A medicine-cabinet mirror reflection at a moment of emotional discovery.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Reflection composition: a man sits in a dim booth facing a one-way mirror, and his faint reflection overlaps a brightly lit woman on the other side of the glass. Physically accurate reflection angles, strong brightness difference so both layers read clearly, 50mm lens.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A pasted-on-looking double whose mirror image doesn't follow the head, with mismatched angle, brightness or action → build a believable optical relationship: correct eyelines, brightness difference and physically accurate surfaces.
