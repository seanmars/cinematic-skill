# Lead Room

Summary: Leave more space ahead of the subject's gaze or direction of travel so the frame points to "what comes next."

## Narrative Function

- Space ahead represents the future and destination; space behind the head represents the past or a predicament.
- Standard grammar for audience comfort; removing it deliberately creates immediate psychological tension.

## How It Works

- Leave more space on the side the subject faces or moves toward: moving left, leave more on the left.
- Pan with a moving subject to maintain room ahead.
- If the story goal is "arrival," let the subject gradually eat up the space ahead.

## When to Use

- Use: walking, gazes awaiting a response, coverage with forward momentum.
- Avoid: when a character is trapped, a liar is cornered under questioning, or danger is imminent; use Short Siding instead.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Short Siding](short-siding.md) | Pushes the face to the edge it looks toward, with all space behind the head |
| [Rule of Thirds](rule-of-thirds.md) | Off-center framing often produces lead room as a by-product |

## Film Examples

- Since studio-era sound films, classical coverage has treated lead room as default grammar.
- Westerns and road movies extend the space ahead into vast landscapes.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Lead room framing: a cyclist rides from right to left, placed on the right third of the frame with wide open road ahead of him on the left side. Camera pans smoothly to keep the space in front, 50mm lens, bright afternoon light, sense of forward momentum.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Accidental Short Siding: profile pressed against the frame edge with a big stretch of wallpaper behind the head → pan toward the gaze so space returns ahead, unless entrapment is the intent.
