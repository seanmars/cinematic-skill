# Frame Within a Frame

Summary: Doors, windows, arches or mirrors form a second frame inside the frame, defining whether the subject is watched, trapped or displayed.

## Narrative Function

- The inner frame is a boundary that decides who is inside and who is shut out.
- Shows the subject being watched, trapped, or gazed at like a painting.
- Crossing the threshold becomes a meaningful story beat, not mere decoration.

## How It Works

- Position the camera so the frame shows two rectangles at once: the outer frame and an architectural opening inside it (door, window, arch, mirror, gap).
- At least three sides of the inner frame must be clearly readable.
- Control the exposure contrast between inner and outer spaces to decide whether the view looks out or in.
- A moderately wide lens (about 32-40mm) usually establishes both boundaries.

## When to Use

- Use: arrivals, departures, corridor encounters, or scenes where identity is tied closely to architectural space.
- Avoid: padding with a decorative arch behind a centered face.
- The inner frame only works when "crossing the threshold" actually changes the scene.

## Film Examples

- *The Searchers* (1956): Doorway compositions at the opening and ending separate the desert from home.
- *In the Mood for Love* (2000): Door frames and corridors enclose private encounters.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Frame within a frame: seen from inside a dark farmhouse, a doorway forms a bright rectangle around a distant rider in the sunlit desert. The door edges are sharp on three sides, strong exposure contrast between the shadowed interior and the glowing exterior, 35mm lens.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A decorative arch behind a centered portrait, with the subject already filling the frame, so the inner frame does nothing → show at least three sides of the inner frame and let the threshold truly divide two spaces.
