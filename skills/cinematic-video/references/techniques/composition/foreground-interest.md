# Foreground Interest

Summary: Place an object close to the lens between camera and subject to create depth and put the audience "inside" the scene.

## Narrative Function

- Builds spatial depth so the audience feels present.
- Can express power relations, an eavesdropping view, or a sense of "not supposed to be here."
- A sharp foreground implies authority and information; a blurred foreground leans voyeuristic.

## How It Works

- Place a specific object on the plane near the lens (flowers, railings, glass, furniture, part of a body) that reads as an obstacle.
- Deliberately position the object's edge to partially cover the subject.
- Wide or moderately wide lenses (24-40mm) amplify the presence of the foreground layer.
- Open up for a hazy mask; stop down so the foreground object is also legible.

## When to Use

- Use: when the camera should feel inside a lived-in space, observing covertly, or when a power-symbol object should sit between audience and subject.
- Avoid: foreground accidentally covering a key expression.
- Avoid: random clutter unrelated to the story blocking the frame.

## Film Examples

- *In the Mood for Love* (2000): Characters glimpsed through doorways, walls and architectural gaps.
- *The Graduate* (1967): Benjamin framed by Mrs. Robinson's bent leg.
- *Citizen Kane* (1941): Objects and tables fill the near plane while action unfolds behind.
- *The Revenant* (2015): Grass and exhaled breath become near-plane obstacles.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Foreground interest: out-of-focus wine glasses and a candle occupy the lower-left corner close to the lens, partially hiding a couple arguing at a dinner table in the midground. 32mm lens, shallow focus on the couple, the foreground blur creating a sense of eavesdropping.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A menu board, sign or passerby's head cuts across a mouth unintentionally, with no narrative purpose → actively choose the foreground object, place its edge, and decide focus depth deliberately.
