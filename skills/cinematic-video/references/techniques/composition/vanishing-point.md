# Vanishing Point

Summary: Parallel lines converge to a point in the distance, turning "distance" and "destination" into the meaning of the frame.

## Narrative Function

- Creates meaning through distance and fate.
- Convergence point behind the subject → the space holds a future; on the subject → the architecture is aiming at them; off to one side → the subject is overlooked.
- The subject becomes a threshold to the distance rather than an enshrined focal point.

## How It Works

- Position the camera where real parallel lines extend into the distance (rails, highways, colonnades).
- Change camera height to adjust the horizon position.
- Decide whether the subject is on, in front of, or beside the convergence point.
- A wide lens strengthens the pull toward infinity.

## When to Use

- Use: when distance itself carries the story, such as roads, stations, landing sites, roads not taken.
- Avoid: flat, shallow spaces with no extending parallel lines or depth layers.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [One-Point Perspective](one-point-perspective.md) | A single axial vanishing point with the camera squared up; vanishing point composition can also use two-point perspective |
| [Leading Lines](leading-lines.md) | Guides the eye but doesn't necessarily converge within the frame |

## Film Examples

- *Paris, Texas* (1984): Highways and rails point to an endless horizon.
- *Close Encounters of the Third Kind* (1977): The perspective of the landing site forms the endpoint of "arrival."

## Prompt Examples

```text
Vanishing point composition: a desert highway and power lines recede to a single point on the low horizon, a lone hitchhiker standing just off the road in the foreground, the convergence point far beyond him. Wide 24mm lens, low camera height, hot hazy afternoon.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A centered person on flat ground, claiming a vanishing point without any converging lines → add real extending parallel lines, or switch to Centered Composition.
