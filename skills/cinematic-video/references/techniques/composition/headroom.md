# Headroom

Summary: Control the distance from the top of the head to the top edge of the frame; tight creates pressure, loose lets the space above take over.

## Narrative Function

- The amount of headroom directly conveys psychological pressure or openness.
- Minimal space feels trapped; with ample space, architecture, sky and emptiness become compositional partners or even characters.

## How It Works

- Measure from the top of the hair (not the eyes) to the top edge of the frame.
- Tight → tension; loose → the upper area dominates the frame.
- Adjust by tilting or booming, no lens change needed.

## When to Use

- Use tight: claustrophobic, no-escape situations.
- Use loose: when landscape, religious imagery or weather should visually overwhelm the character.
- Avoid: unintended extra space from forgetting to tilt.

## Film Examples

- *Ida* (2013): Faces pressed to the bottom of the frame beneath large empty areas.
- *There Will Be Blood* (2007): Oil derricks and sky dwarf the characters.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Generous headroom: a nun's face sits at the very bottom of the frame, while a vast gray cathedral ceiling and cold sky fill the upper two-thirds above her. Static medium shot, black-and-white, the empty space above feeling heavy and spiritual.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Accidental sinking: forgetting to tilt, the character drops low and a large stretch of ceiling is wasted → tilt down for ordinary portraits; to keep it, deliberately place the face low and design it as negative space.
