# Asymmetry

Summary: The subject sits to one side while color blocks, light sources or objects counterweight the other, giving a balanced frame with tension.

## Narrative Function

- Builds spatial tension and unequal power relations.
- The protagonist occupies a small area while color, light or objects fill the rest; often used for desire, class and loneliness.

## How It Works

- Place the subject off-center, then counterweight with practicals, secondary characters, color blocks or architectural elements.
- Watch the monitor without guide lines; if it feels unbalanced, adjust mass or cropping.
- A slightly wide lens (about 32-40mm) makes the empty area read as deliberate.

## When to Use

- Use: scenes of power imbalance, loneliness or emotional strain.
- Avoid: as a default messy placement; every offset needs a matching counterweight.
- Avoid: when the scene needs ceremony or symmetrical order.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Rule of Thirds](rule-of-thirds.md) | A specific grid-based offset; asymmetry is the larger family |

## Film Examples

- *In the Mood for Love* (2000): Characters are squeezed into corridors and doorways; wallpaper and lamps outweigh faces.
- *Drive* (2011): The weight of night, steel and neon overwhelms the still driver.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Asymmetrical composition: a woman stands small at the far left edge of a narrow hallway, while a glowing red lamp and patterned wallpaper fill the right two-thirds as a heavy counterweight. 35mm lens, moody tungsten light, quiet tension, no mirrored balance.
```

## Common Mistakes

- The subject is offset but nothing on the other side answers it, so it just looks crooked → add a real counterweight, move the subject to an intersection, or switch to full symmetry.
