# Windows

Summary: A grid of windows on a building facade shows many slices of life at once, so a single shot works like a montage.

## Narrative Function

- Makes the architectural grid, not a single character, the subject.
- Presents multiple lives simultaneously in one shot, forming an in-shot montage.
- Shows how different people share one space, or makes a character just one cell among many.

## How It Works

- Find a real grid structure (courtyard, apartment block, office tower).
- A long lens compresses the panes into a flat plane; or shoot from inside out with a wide lens.
- Expose so multiple interiors are visible at once, or deliberately darken the other rooms and keep only the important one.
- The building itself becomes the "editing device."

## When to Use

- Use: depicting many residents sharing one building, or showing a single character as one cell among many compartments.
- Avoid: when only a single encounter between two people is needed; that calls for a single frame within a frame.

## Film Examples

- *Rear Window* (1954): The courtyard is transformed into multiple vignettes playing out simultaneously.
- *The Hours* (2002): Windows become thresholds between inner psychology and the urban environment.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Windows composition: a flat telephoto view of an apartment building facade at dusk, a grid of lit windows each showing a different life, a couple dancing, a man eating alone, an empty room with a lamp, a child at a desk. Building as a mosaic of rectangles, warm interior light against cool blue exterior.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Just one rainy window portrait, or a single-window doorframe composition with no other active cells → reveal multiple openings and make sure at least one other cell shows signs of life (lights, empty furniture).
