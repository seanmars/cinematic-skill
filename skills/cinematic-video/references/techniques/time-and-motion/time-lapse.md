# Time-Lapse

Summary: Shot at fixed intervals, compressing a slow process into fast footage and revealing patterns invisible to the naked eye.

## Narrative Function

- Reveals patterns invisible in real-time viewing, such as weather, construction or crowds.
- People become part of a system rather than protagonists.
- Usually paired with a locked-off camera.

## How It Works

- Plan the shooting interval, total duration, exposure and camera stability.
- For day-to-night shots, adjust exposure gradually as the sky darkens and windows light up (exposure ramp).
- The sun and shadows must move across the frame to support the claim that "time is passing".

## When to Use

- Use: Weather changes, construction progress and nightfall from a fixed viewpoint.
- Avoid: Speeding up actor performances, or comedic undercranking.

## Film Examples

- *Koyaanisqatsi* (1982): Urban cycles, traffic and crowds turned into large-scale visual patterns.
- *Baraka* (1992): Organizes cities and human rituals across different timescales.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Locked-off time-lapse of a city skyline from late afternoon to night: shadows sweep across the buildings, the sky darkens through dusk, and office windows light up one by one. Traffic flows as streaks of light, architecture stays perfectly stable, consistent light logic throughout.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Sun not moving, pedestrians blurred together, buildings warping → let shadows sweep across the frame, keep the composition stable and advance the daylight phases continuously.
- Assuming crowds alone are enough → changes in light and shadow are needed to prove time is passing.
