# Reverse Motion

Summary: Recorded action plays backward, so effect precedes cause and the laws of physics visibly invert.

## Narrative Function

- Makes "effect" appear before "cause" within a single shot.
- Creates a visual "restoration" or magic-spell effect.
- Conveys impossible physics and reversed inertia through the movement of water, fabric and debris.

## How It Works

- Shoot an action with clear physical interaction forward, then reverse the footage.
- The behavior of gravity, liquid and fabric is the visual evidence of "reversal".
- When describing it, specify that objects reassemble or rise against natural law.

## When to Use

- Use: Shots where objects must reassemble or liquid visibly flows upward.
- Use: Expressing grief that longs for reversal, or an alternate set of physical laws.
- Avoid: Reverse-chronology structures played at normal speed, or simple looping animation.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Boomerang](boomerang.md) | Forward first, then back along the same path |
| [Slow Motion](slow-motion.md) | Changes only speed, not direction |
| [Infinite Loop](infinite-loop.md) | The last frame joins back to the first, always forward |

## Film Examples

- *Memento* (2000): The opening Polaroid photo and violent action play backward.
- *Tenet* (2020): Objects and people move in an inverted direction of time.
- *Irréversible* (2002): The main sequences are arranged in reverse order (this is a reverse-chronology structure, not reversed footage).

## Prompt Examples

```text
Reverse motion playback: shattered pieces of a ceramic vase fly up off the floor and reassemble in the woman's hands, spilled water flows back up into it, cigarette smoke is drawn back into her lips. One continuous shot playing backward, physics visibly inverted, not a reordering of scenes.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Treating a reverse-chronology structure (scene order reversed) as reverse motion → what is reversed is the recorded action itself.
- Not readable as playing backward → add obvious physics-defying cues such as liquid, debris or smoke.
