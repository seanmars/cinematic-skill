# Bullet Time

Summary: The action freezes or nearly freezes while the camera moves around it, turning an instant into an explorable space.

## Narrative Function

- Turns a split-second moment into an inspectable space.
- Offers a viewpoint no real observer in the scene could have.
- Also called time-slice orbit or The Matrix effect.

## How It Works

- Done with a synchronized multi-camera array plus frame interpolation, or in a fully controlled 3D scene.
- Subject geometry stays frozen or nearly frozen; only the viewpoint changes.
- Particles must stay countable and fixed throughout the arc.

## When to Use

- Use: Inspecting the moment of an impact, jump or dodge (e.g. dodging bullets on a rooftop).
- Avoid: Dialogue scenes, leisurely walking, or impact close-ups with a locked-off camera.
- Avoid: Cases where the camera is static or the subject is still moving slowly; that is freeze frame or slow motion.

## Film Examples

- *The Matrix* (1999): Neo leans back to dodge bullets on a rooftop as the camera circles around him.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Bullet time: a fighter hangs mid-air in a frozen spinning kick, shattered glass and dust suspended rigidly around her. The camera glides in a smooth 180-degree arc around the frozen moment; time for the subject has stopped while the viewpoint keeps moving, shadows stay consistent.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Handheld slow motion passed off as bullet time, with the camera not orbiting or the subject still moving → freeze or nearly freeze the subject and orbit the camera along a specified arc.
- Suspended debris jitters, shadows are inconsistent, limbs duplicate → keep particles fixed, shadows continuous and anatomy consistent.
