# Stop Motion

Summary: A physical puppet or model is adjusted slightly for each frame shot, building up motion frame by frame.

## Narrative Function

- Presents a handcrafted world where texture, weight and tactile materials are themselves the performance.
- Suits puppets, miniatures and sculpted objects.
- Not suited as a jitter filter applied to live actors.

## How It Works

- Lock the camera position, keep lighting consistent and plan the pose spacing.
- Secure models to prevent shifting; replacement animation (swapping parts such as mouth shapes frame by frame) can be used.
- Keep joints and deliberate pose increments clearly visible.

## When to Use

- Use: When puppets or objects should perform like photographed sculptures.
- Avoid: Frame-skipping effects on live actors, or step printing on street scenes.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Step Printing](step-printing.md) | Reprints live-action frames; stop motion moves physical models |

## Film Examples

- *King Kong* (1933): A physical creature model driven by animation.
- *Jason and the Argonauts* (1963): The skeleton battle, combining animated characters with live performance.
- *Coraline* (2009): A world built from puppets and miniature sets.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Stop-motion animation: a felt-and-clay fox puppet walks across a miniature cardboard forest in slightly stepped, pose-to-pose increments, with visible fabric texture and fingerprint marks. Locked camera, steady repeatable lighting, clear contact between paws and ground, no fluid organic motion.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Materials deforming, paper melting, hair flowing; actually mislabeled live-action jitter → keep models intact and pose transitions deliberate.
- Camera position or lighting drifts → lock the camera and keep lighting repeatable.
