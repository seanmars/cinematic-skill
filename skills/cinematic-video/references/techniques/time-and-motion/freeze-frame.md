# Freeze Frame

Summary: Time stops on a single frame; image and grain go completely still while sound can continue.

## Narrative Function

- Used to close a scene, land a punchline or present a face as "evidence".
- The image is still, but narration or music can keep going.

## How It Works

- Pick one frame and hold it for the required length.
- An optical push-in on the still image is "moving over a photo", not new live-action movement.

## When to Use

- Use: Endings, comedic punctuation, moments that need to underline evidence.
- Avoid: Shots meant to have suspended particles while the camera moves.
- Avoid: When the meaning of the dialogue depends on the next word; or when the camera needs to move around a stopped body.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Slow Motion](slow-motion.md) | Still keeps sampling the action, at a slower rate |
| [Frozen in Motion](frozen-in-motion.md) | The world is still, but some elements keep moving |
| [Bullet Time](bullet-time.md) | The camera orbits a frozen moment |
| [Tableau](../composition/tableau.md) | A still arrangement staged within flowing time |

## Film Examples

- *The 400 Blows* (1959): The ending freezes Antoine at the seashore.
- *Goodfellas* (1990): Henry freezes mid-action while the narration introduces him.
- *Rocky* (1976): A still punctuation at the moment of victory.
- *Trainspotting* (1996): Freeze-frame hits synced to the song in the opening.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Freeze frame: the teenager turns toward the camera mid-stride and the image suddenly stops dead on a single frame, film grain frozen with it. The camera and the entire world stay completely still, no drifting debris and no camera orbit, while music continues underneath.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confused with frozen in motion or bullet time → both the camera and the whole world stop on the same frame.
- Using a freeze frame when an orbiting move is needed → use bullet time instead.
