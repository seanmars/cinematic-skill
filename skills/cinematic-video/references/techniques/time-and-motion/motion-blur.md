# Motion Blur

Summary: The subject or camera moves during exposure, smearing the image and turning light sources into streaks.

## Narrative Function

- Uses smearing and softening to express speed, trauma or disorientation.
- Edges are deliberately blurred, trading clarity for a sense of motion.

## How It Works

- A longer shutter exposure combined with movement smears the image.
- Light sources become ribbon-like light trails; fast-moving subjects blur together.
- The camera can be static with the subject moving, or the reverse.

## When to Use

- Use: Light trails at night, panicked camera moves, whip effects.
- Avoid: Staccato combat that needs a frame-by-frame fractured feel.
- Avoid: Adding blur in post on top of crisp footage.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Whip Pan](../camera-movement/whip-pan.md) | Fast rotation that wipes out the in-between frames |
| [Low Shutter](low-shutter.md) | Narrow shutter angle, producing a sharp, staccato effect |
| [Slow Motion](slow-motion.md) | Usually sharper instead, at high frame rates |

## Film Examples

- *Cloverfield* (2008): Handheld panic blurs the monster and streets together.
- *Bourne* series (2002-2007): Bursts of heavy whip-pans during action.
- *Saving Private Ryan* (1998): Mainly short-shutter staccato rather than smearing (counter-example).

## Prompt Examples

```text
Strong motion blur from a dragged shutter: a motorcyclist races through a rain-soaked city at night, headlights and neon signs stretching into long ribbons of light. His body and the passing crowd smear across the frame, streaks instead of crisp raindrops.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confused with a narrow shutter angle, producing crisp raindrops and fractured limbs → lengthen the shutter or increase movement until light sources draw trails.
- Adding blur in post on sharp footage → let the blur come from real movement during exposure.
