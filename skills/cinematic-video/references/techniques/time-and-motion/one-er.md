# Oner

Summary: Looks like a single continuous shot, flowing through multiple connected spaces and actions; hidden stitches are acceptable.

## Narrative Function

- Makes continuous space and action feel done in one breath, creating a smooth sense of flow.
- The focus is on the "journey" and overall choreography of blocking, not on proving it is truly uncut.

## How It Works

- First plan an executable camera path, then find where stitches can be hidden.
- Lighting, eyelines and performance energy must match on both sides of each stitch.
- Steadicam, crane or handheld all work, as long as the camera's "breathing" stays steady.

## When to Use

- Use: Scenes that need to pass through multiple connected locations, such as backstage or a rescue breakout.
- Avoid: Static two-person dialogue, where basic coverage is enough.
- Avoid: When "truly uncut" is itself a promise the work must keep (use a long take instead).

## Film Examples

- *Birdman* (2014): Backstage chaos seems done in one breath but actually hides stitches.
- *1917* (2019): Trenches and fields strung into one continuous war journey, also stitched.
- *Extraction* (2020): Apartment and vehicle action designed as a single-shot sequence.
- *Russian Ark* (2002): A truly uncut feature, useful as a contrast to the stitched oner.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Seamless oner: the camera follows a stage manager from the dressing room, down a narrow corridor, left through the wings and out onto the lit stage in one fluid movement. Invisible transitions, consistent lighting and energy throughout, the character always the anchor of the frame.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Ordinary coverage passed off as a oner, or a long take with covert cuts → maintain one visible, continuous path.
- Insert shots exposing the stitches, mismatched energy across a stitch → remove the inserts and match action and energy on both sides of the stitch.
