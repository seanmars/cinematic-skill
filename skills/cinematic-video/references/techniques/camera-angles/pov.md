# Point of View

Summary: The camera is the character's eyes; the audience "becomes" them and sees what they see.

## Narrative Function

- Forces the audience to identify with the character, occupying their line of sight rather than watching from behind.
- Forms an editing pattern: character looks → we see what they see → often cut back to their reaction.

## How It Works

- Camera at the character's eye height, aligned with their eyeline.
- Focal length matches the state of attention: long lens for focused observation, wide lens for frantic scanning.
- Key: The viewer's head and shoulders must never enter the frame.

## When to Use

- Use: After a character looks off-screen, to let the audience share their perception; concrete moments of threat or discovery.
- Avoid: Using it as the only coverage for a conversation.
- Avoid: When the viewer's face must also be seen.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Over-the-Shoulder Frame](../framing/over-the-shoulder-frame.md) | Keeps the near shoulder and far face; the audience is "with" the character rather than "becoming" them |
| [First-Person](first-person.md) | Extended ownership: hands, breath and movement visible, sustained over time |
| [Object POV](object-pov.md) | Seen by a tool or vehicle, not a person |
| [Fourth Wall](fourth-wall.md) | Reversed: the character looks directly into the audience's lens |

## Film Examples

- *Rear Window* (1954): Courtyard views aligned with the telephoto lens, synced with Jeff's observation.
- *Halloween* (1978): The opening plays from the killer's point of view before revealing his identity.
- *Saving Private Ryan* (1998): Miller's hearing drops out during the beach landing, reflecting perceptual shock.
- *Gravity* (2013): Helmet-height subjectivity merges with the surrounding space.

## Prompt Examples

```text
POV shot through the eyes of a night guard at standing eye height, looking down a dim warehouse aisle toward a half-open door at the far end. No head, shoulder or body of the viewer visible; the camera slowly pans right as if the guard turns toward a sudden noise.
```

## Common Mistakes

- A blurry shoulder, the back of a head, or the viewer's face turned toward the lens appears → Remove the viewer's body completely and point the line of sight into the scene.
- Height inconsistent with the character's height in earlier close-ups → Match camera height to the character's eye height.
