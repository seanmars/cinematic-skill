# Hip Level

Summary: The lens sits at a standing character's belt / holster height, making hands and weapons as prominent as faces.

## Narrative Function

- Originating in westerns, it emphasizes hand position and weapons in standoffs.
- Shifts focus from faces to fingers and belt buckles, building tension before conflict erupts.
- Crime films borrow it to hint at hidden weapons in dialogue scenes.

## How It Works

- For a standing character, the camera is about 90-110 cm high.
- Horizon and roll stay level; a slight upward tilt is allowed.
- Depth of field must keep both the holster and the opposing body sharp; wide lenses elongate legs, long lenses compress.

## When to Use

- Use: Moments where hands decide the outcome, such as standoffs, quick draws, reaching into pockets or waist-level exchanges.
- Avoid: Face-driven dialogue (use a higher camera position).

## Film Examples

- *The Good, the Bad and the Ugly* (1966): In standoffs, holsters and hands are in focus.
- *No Country for Old Men* (2007): Framed at belt and pocket height; weapons are as prominent as faces.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Hip-level shot with the camera at belt height between two standing gunfighters, one man's hand resting near his holster in sharp focus in the foreground, the other's face visible near the top of frame. Level horizon, no upward tilt, deep enough focus to hold both figures.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confused with the power of a low angle, or shooting a mid-thigh-up frame from eye height (nostrils / ceiling visible) → Camera at belt height, line of sight through the hands, level horizon.
- Only the boots are sharp → It has dropped to ground level; raise the camera back to the waist.
