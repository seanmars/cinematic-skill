# Bird's-Eye View

Summary: Looking almost straight down from high above, turning people and objects into symbols and patterns on a map.

## Narrative Function

- Turns people and objects into abstract patterns and game pieces, emphasizing spatial relationships, crowds and ritual.
- Reveals structures invisible to those inside them.
- Faces and dialogue are no longer the focus.

## How It Works

- Camera placed far above the action, pointing down at 70-90 degrees.
- Bodies are heavily foreshortened; heads become circles.
- Raking top light casts clear shadows across the ground.

## When to Use

- Use: When composition, blocking and ritual arrangement matter more than expression and dialogue.
- Avoid: Dialogue-driven or intimate scenes.
- Avoid: When you only want to show "a small person in a room" (a high angle is enough).

## Film Examples

- *Requiem for a Dream* (2000): Height presents the apartment and bodies as patterns of habit.
- *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (2014): Steep top-down views turn the hotel and its rituals into architectural diagrams.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Bird's-eye view from high above a town square, camera pointing nearly straight down at about 80 degrees, people reduced to round heads and long raking shadows. A wedding procession forms a clear curving pattern across the cobblestones.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Not high enough, ending up as a mild high angle → Raise the camera and steepen the angle so bodies foreshorten and the ground becomes geography.
- Overhead shot too close, looking like product photography → Keep distance and clear stray limbs from seated group shots.
