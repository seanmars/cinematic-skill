# Profile

Summary: The camera sits at 90 degrees to the face, showing only one eye and one ear, with the bridge of the nose defining the outline.

## Narrative Function

- Hides one eye and refuses direct engagement with the audience, creating mystery and formality.
- Keeps emotion accessible while implying concealment or authority.

## How It Works

- Camera perpendicular to the face direction (90-degree yaw), usually at eye height.
- Only one ear is visible, the nose is a prominent structural element, and the eyeline points off-screen away from the lens.
- Light with grazing light to separate features, or render it as a silhouette.

## When to Use

- Use: Statuesque feel, secrets, power dynamics, formal authority figures.
- Avoid: Dialogue needing emotional transparency, intimate moments.
- Avoid: Conversations that need both eyes visible to be believable.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Three-Quarter](three-quarter.md) | About 45 degrees; both eyes visible, far cheek recedes |
| [Eye Level](eye-level.md) | Describes height, not facing; a profile is usually eye height + 90-degree yaw |
| [POV](pov.md) | Shows what the subject sees, not their profile |
| [Fourth Wall](fourth-wall.md) | Eyes look directly into the lens, the opposite of a profile looking off-screen |
| [High Angle](high-angle.md) | Conveys vulnerability or surveillance, not concealment |

## Film Examples

- *The Godfather* (1972): Vito at his desk faces petitioners in profile rather than facing the audience.
- *Barry Lyndon* (1975): Profile compositions like historical portrait paintings that still retain humanity.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Strict profile shot of an old judge at eye level, camera at a true 90 degrees to his face so the far eye is completely hidden and the nose breaks the cheek line. He gazes off-screen right, a hard grazing side light carving the skull's volume against a dark wall.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Accidentally shot as a three-quarter (far pupil visible, cheek still facing the lens) → Keep turning until the far eye disappears completely and the nose cuts across the cheek line.
- Profile as flat as a paper cutout → Retain the skull's three-dimensional volume and skin texture.
