# Reverse Angle

Summary: Moves the camera to the other side of the axis so the audience sees whom the character is looking at or talking to.

## Narrative Function

- Completes the axis of a gaze, conversation or confrontation, revealing what the character sees.
- Spatial orientation flips, but screen direction stays consistent.

## How It Works

- Move the camera to the opposite side without crossing the 180-degree line.
- Match lens height and shot size to the first shot.
- Both eyelines must pass through the same empty space between them.

## When to Use

- Use: When the audience needs to see the other participant in an exchange.
- Avoid: When the scene's tension relies on "deliberately withholding" the object of the gaze.

## Film Examples

- *Neighbors* (1920): An early demonstration of maintaining spatial orientation.
- *Tokyo Story* (1953): An alternative system of axis continuity.
- *Rosemary's Baby* (1968): Delays the reverse, keeping the audience in a state of incomplete information.
- *The Shining* (1980): The red bathroom conversation uses unsettling opposing shots.
- *Heat* (1995): The diner scene maintains opposite screen directions.
- *The Blair Witch Project* (1999): Deliberately withholds the reverse, creating menace by "refusing to show."

## Prompt Examples

```text
Reverse angle answering a previous medium close-up of a detective: now framing the suspect from the opposite side of the 180-degree line, same lens height and same shot size. The suspect looks screen-left while the detective had looked screen-right, the interrogation room's window now visible behind him.
```

## Common Mistakes

- The new setup crosses the 180-degree line, or shot sizes mismatch (answering a close-up with a wide shot) → Keep opposite eyelines, the same scale and height, and respect the 180-degree line.
