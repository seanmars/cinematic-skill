# Dutch Angle

Summary: The camera rotates around the lens axis, tilting the horizon and verticals so the world loses its balance.

## Narrative Function

- Creates visual disorientation, conveying moral corruption, mental instability or a world out of order.
- The effect comes from composition (roll), not camera height or power dynamics.
- Also called canted angle, Dutch tilt, oblique angle.

## How It Works

- Rotate 15-30 degrees around the lens axis; beyond 45 degrees it often reads as a mistake.
- Deliberately arrange diagonal elements such as stairs, railings and streets.
- Architecture stays rigid and undistorted; the unease comes from the viewpoint, not warped geometry.

## When to Use

- Use: To show psychological or moral disorder.
- Avoid: Using it for every villain or every night scene; contrast and meaning disappear.
- Works best when adjacent shots stay level.

## Film Examples

- *The Third Man* (1949): Streets and encounters in postwar Vienna use canted framing extensively.
- *Do the Right Thing* (1989): Used only in specific moments of conflict, not across the whole film.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Static Dutch angle with the camera rolled about 25 degrees, the horizon and a staircase railing cutting diagonally across frame. A man waits at the bottom of the stairs, walls straight and undistorted, lens held at eye height with no upward pitch and no rolling motion.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confused with a low angle (ceiling visible but horizon level) → Keep pitch level and add only 20-30 degrees of roll.
- Wanting a static tilt but getting a Dutch roll → Lock off the camera; don't rotate during the take.
- Every shot tilted → Keep reverse shots and adjacent shots level for contrast.
