# High Angle

Summary: The camera sits above eye level looking obliquely down; the character shrinks but the face stays clear as the floor and spatial layout emerge.

## Narrative Function

- Conveys vulnerability, surveillance or spatial hierarchy.
- Makes the subject look small in the environment while the face and room geometry remain readable.

## How It Works

- Camera above the subject's eyes, angled down 20-45 degrees.
- Horizon stays level; an oblique view, not straight down.
- Camera near the ceiling; light often comes from overhead practicals.

## When to Use

- Use: Moments of vulnerability or "being watched."
- Use: To reveal floor layouts and spatial relationships invisible at eye level.
- Avoid: When the subject needs an equal power position, or when a true top-down floor plan is needed.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Eye Level](eye-level.md) | Neutral view, no implied hierarchy |
| [Bird's-Eye View](birds-eye.md) | Higher and steeper; people become diagram symbols |

## Film Examples

- *Psycho* (1960): The staircase scene first places the character within the house's geometry before the attack.
- *The Shawshank Redemption* (1994): Top-down prison views emphasize the confined scale of people before the architecture.

## Prompt Examples

```text
High-angle shot looking down at about 30 degrees on a child sitting alone on a hospital corridor bench, face still readable, the long tiled floor and receding walls filling the frame around her. Level horizon, cool overhead fluorescent light.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Confused with bird's-eye or overhead (only the top of the head visible, tabletops become rectangles) → Keep about a 30-degree downward angle with a readable face and receding walls.
