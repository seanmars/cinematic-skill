# Low Angle

Summary: The camera sits below eye level looking up, so the character towers like a building, bringing authority or menace.

## Narrative Function

- Turns the subject into a monumental or threatening presence.
- Brings in architectural elements such as ceilings and door frames.
- Expresses power, dominance and intimidation through geometry, not just performance.

## How It Works

- Camera below the subject's eyes, tilted up until verticals converge toward the top of frame.
- Wide lenses exaggerate convergence; long lenses distort less.
- Keep roll level unless deliberately combining with a Dutch angle.

## When to Use

- Use: To show authority or menace, such as a standing figure looming over a seated listener.
- Avoid: Using it as the default angle to glamorize the protagonist.
- Avoid: Conversations between equals that need equal visual status.

## Film Examples

- *Citizen Kane* (1941): Ceilings and encroaching bodies form confrontational geometry.
- *Touch of Evil* (1958): Low angles amplify Quinlan's oppressive bulk.
- *Late Spring* (1949) / *Tokyo Story* (1953): Often mistaken for low angles, but actually low camera height + level line of sight.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Low-angle shot from below chest height tilting up at a factory foreman standing over a seated worker, verticals of the steel beams converging toward the top of frame, ceiling visible above him. Face still clearly readable, level roll, 28mm lens.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Shot as a worm's-eye view (only nostrils and sky) or a ground-level boot shot → Keep the face readable, include the ceiling, use a moderate upward angle.
- Treating Ozu-style low, level shots as low angles → A true low angle must tilt up.
