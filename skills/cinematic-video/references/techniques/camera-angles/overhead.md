# Overhead Top-Down

Summary: The lens points straight down at 90 degrees, flattening the scene into a floor plan with people as graphic elements in the composition.

## Narrative Function

- Turns the scene into a floor plan or system: crime scenes, table settings, dance formations.
- Removes the social cues of faces; people are only visual elements within a structure.
- Shadows themselves become compositional lines.

## How It Works

- Camera mounted on an overhead rig or lighting grid, pointing straight down (90 degrees).
- Faces are hard to see, so block the action for clear silhouettes.
- Identify characters by costume color and contrast.

## When to Use

- Use: Choreography, evidence layouts and ritual moments that read as diagrams.
- Avoid: Scenes driven by dialogue or facial expression.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Bird's-Eye View](birds-eye.md) | Shot from extreme height with a sense of altitude; overhead is closer and more geometric |
| [High Angle](high-angle.md) | Oblique downward view, faces readable; overhead is strictly vertical |

## Film Examples

- *Dogville* (2003): Presents the entire town as a floor plan on the ground.
- *The Grand Budapest Hotel* (2014): Directly overhead compositions of tabletops and pastries.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Overhead top-down shot pointing straight down at a kitchen table mid-dinner, the tabletop a perfect rectangle with no perspective taper. Four diners seen only as the tops of their heads and outstretched arms, plates and colored napkins forming a clean graphic layout, camera not rotated.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Shot as a steep high angle with walls and faces still in perspective (table becomes a trapezoid, eyelines visible) → Achieve a true 90 degrees, keep geometry rectangular and silhouettes clear.
