# Incline

Summary: The horizon tilts only a few degrees, quietly hinting at unease rather than loudly announcing it like a Dutch angle.

## Narrative Function

- Quietly suggests unease or a sense of slope, making the space feel "slightly off."
- Hints at tension in a hallway or conversation without a strong graphic statement.

## How It Works

- Roll the camera 5-12 degrees and keep it static.
- Architecture and bodies stay clearly readable; only the frame tilts.
- Avoid fisheye and other ultra-wide lenses, which swallow this subtle effect.

## When to Use

- Use: Hallways, mild conflict, streets that need an "almost wrong" feel.
- Avoid: When you need the obvious tilt of a Dutch angle.
- Avoid: When building an actual slope would work better.

## Compared With Similar Techniques

| Technique | Difference |
| --- | --- |
| [Dutch Angle](dutch-angle.md) | 20-30 degree tilt; clearly expresses moral or mental imbalance |
| [Dutch Roll](../camera-movement/dutch-roll.md) | A roll that moves over time |

## Film Examples

- *The Third Man* (1949): Uses varying degrees of tilt; the shallower ones are inclines, the heavier ones Dutch.
- *Do the Right Thing* (1989): Gradually adjusts the tilt angle as conflict escalates.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Static shot of a quiet apartment hallway with the camera rolled only about 8 degrees, horizon just slightly off true. Doors and walls stay rigid and readable, a man waits at the far end; subtle unease, not a dramatic Dutch tilt, no camera motion.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Tilted 25 degrees or more, becoming a full Dutch angle → Keep it at 5-12 degrees, barely noticeable, with the horizon only slightly off.
