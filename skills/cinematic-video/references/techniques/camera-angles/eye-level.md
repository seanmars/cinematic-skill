# Eye Level

Summary: The camera sits at the subject's eye height with a level line of sight and straight horizon, presenting an equal, neutral view.

## Narrative Function

- Assigns no rank to characters, establishing an equal relationship.
- Leaves meaning to performance, wardrobe and lighting rather than camera geometry.
- Unsuited to surveillance, monumental or map-like views.

## How It Works

- Lens nodal point at the subject's eye height, zero roll, verticals kept vertical.
- 35-50mm approximates human vision.
- Height and pitch are independent variables: correct height with tilt becomes a different angle.

## When to Use

- Use: Conversations between equals that need neutrality and believable faces.
- Avoid: When you need to show vulnerability (shoot from above) or dominance (shoot from below).
- Match camera height to the subject's posture: if the subject sits, lower the camera to seated eye height.

## Film Examples

- *Tokyo Story* (1953): Family conversations stay at human height.
- *Rosetta* (1999): The camera follows the protagonist at eye height, refusing to look down.

## Prompt Examples

```text
Eye-level shot of two coworkers seated across a small table, camera lowered to their seated eye height, perfectly level horizon and no tilt up or down. 40mm lens, neutral framing that favors neither person.
```

## Common Mistakes

- Generation defaults to a slight low angle (ceiling visible, chin seen from below, converging verticals) → Explicitly specify eye height and a level horizon; lower the camera when the subject is seated.
